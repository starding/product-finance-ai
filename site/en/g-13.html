Chapter 13

A new parameter: the cost of cognitive labour collapsesWhich part of the cost falls

A group of scribes has arrived on the island. They can read every fleet's report, work out every household's accounts and draft every letter; they ask almost nothing in return and never tire. The price of having someone read, calculate and write on your behalf has suddenly fallen to a few hundredths of what it was, or less. But the cost of a delegation is not made up only of this kind of labour. Someone still has to check whether the scribe got it right, and someone has to bear responsibility when things go wrong, while the scribes themselves have neither property to pledge nor a name built up over years. Their abilities are also uneven: some tasks that look hard they do very well, while some that look easy they get wrong. Once the cost of cognitive labour collapses, which part of the cost has actually fallen?

1. Scribes arrive on the island

Up to this point, every institution on the island has depended on one kind of labour. Fleet managers gather news and compare ships; custodians check accounts; those selling ship shares find out what buyers can bear; those designing defaults estimate what most people want. All of this is the labour of reading, calculating, judging and writing. Until now only people could do it, and it was expensive.

Now a group of scribes has arrived on the island, and they do this work for almost nothing. There is one new constraint: the price of cognitive labour collapses, and everything else stays the same.

Today's counterpart. Stanford's AI Index Report 2025 estimates that the price of calling a large model at a given level of performance fell from about US$20 per million tokens in November 2022 to about US$0.07 in October 2024, a drop of more than 280-fold.

2. What the cost of a delegation is made of

When a principal hires a fleet manager to look after ship shares, what the principal pays for is not just the manager's time spent studying ships. Broken down, the cost has roughly five parts: research and comparison; drafting and judgement; carrying out instructions; checking what the manager did; and bearing responsibility when something goes wrong. The first three are mainly cognitive labour. Part of the checking can be handed to scribes, but whoever signs off at the end still has to look for themselves. Bearing responsibility requires someone with property that can pay compensation and a reputation that can be lost.

The scribes make the first three parts almost free, but the last two do not fall with them. The cheaper cognitive labour becomes, the larger the share of a delegation's cost that goes to checking and bearing responsibility.

Today's counterpart. In investment advisory services, research reports and portfolio recommendations can be generated by software, but suitability judgements made for clients, reconciliation and liability for compensation still rest with licensed firms and staff.

3. Evidence from experiments

How much can the scribes actually save? Several recent experiments give preliminary answers. Noy and Zhang (2023) had 453 college-educated professionals complete writing tasks from their own occupations, randomly allowing half of them to use a conversational large model: average time taken fell by 40% and quality rose by 18%. Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond (2025) studied 5,172 customer-support agents who gained access to an AI assistant in stages: issues resolved per hour rose by 15% on average, with large differences between individuals. The earlier working paper reported that novices and lower-skilled workers improved by about 34%, while the most experienced saw almost no improvement.

Today's counterpart. These experiments mostly involve short, self-contained tasks, unlike delegation that lasts for years. They show the cost of single pieces of cognitive labour, not the cost of a complete service.

4. An uneven edge

The scribes' abilities do not vary neatly with how hard a task is. Dell'Acqua et al. (2023) ran an experiment at a consulting firm. On tasks within the large model's capabilities, consultants using it completed 12.2% more tasks, worked 25.1% faster and produced work more than 40% higher in quality. On a task that looked similarly difficult but lay outside its capabilities, consultants using it were about 19 percentage points less likely to give the correct answer. The authors call this irregular boundary of capability the "jagged technological frontier".

An uneven edge means that whether to check cannot be decided by how hard a task looks. The most dangerous tasks are precisely those that look easy but which the scribe gets wrong.

Today's counterpart. A large model may perform well on complex reasoning yet make mistakes on simple counting or on citing sources, and it states its errors with the same confidence as its correct answers.

5. The weakest stave: the slowest step sets the limit

Even if the scribes make one part of the work a hundred times faster, the whole job does not become a hundred times faster. Suppose a delegation used to take ten days: nine for reading, calculating and drafting, one for checking and signing. The scribes cut the nine days to a hundredth of their former length, but checking and signing still take a day, so the whole job goes from ten days to about 1.1 days, a little over nine times faster. Even if the nine days were cut to zero, the whole job could be at most ten times faster. The one day that cannot be sped up sets the ceiling for the whole.

Derived so far: a principle

Amdahl's law

DisciplineComputer science › Computer architecture (parallel computing)TypeLaw

When only part of a job can be sped up, the overall speed-up is limited by the part that cannot: if the part that cannot be sped up is a fraction s of the whole, then however much faster the rest becomes, the whole can be at most 1/s times faster.

Standing in the field
Named after its proposer and commonly called "Amdahl's law"; the original is a three-page conference paper with no formula, which estimated in words the speed limit that sequential data-management overhead places on parallel processing
Constraint it answers
Only part of the work can be sped up
Premises
The work can be divided into a part that can be sped up and a part that cannot; the two parts must be done one after the other; the total amount of work is fixed
Proposed by
Amdahl, 1967, in a discussion of the prospects for multiprocessor parallel computing
Open question
Can the part that cannot be sped up be made smaller by redesigning the process?
After the rerun
With cognitive labour almost free, the speed-up of reading, calculating and drafting is in effect pushed towards infinity, so the overall ceiling is set entirely by the share taken up by checking, bearing responsibility and waiting

For delegation, the parts that cannot be sped up are exactly checking, bearing responsibility, and waiting itself: grain is still harvested once a year, and ships still sail for months. With the cost of cognitive labour collapsing, the overall amplification factor depends on whether these steps can change as well.

Today's counterpart. An investment recommendation can be generated in seconds, but account opening, suitability assessment, the arrival of funds and trade settlement each still have their own procedures and timelines.

6. The reachable share

What proportion of all work can the scribes do? Eloundou, Manning, Mishkin and Rock assessed the tasks of each US occupation one by one. Their 2023 preprint estimated that about 80% of workers have at least 10% of their tasks affected by large models, and about 19% of workers have at least half of their tasks affected. The version formally published in 2024 uses a different measure that distinguishes two cases. With a large model used only through a simple interface, occupations with more than half their tasks affected make up about 1.8%; counting the software and tools built on top of large models, the figure rises to more than 46%. The reachable share therefore depends mainly on what is built around the large model, not only on the model itself. The authors also note that being affected is not the same as being replaced, and that being technically feasible is not the same as being adopted.

Today's counterpart. In finance, research, customer service, compliance review and report writing are all jobs whose tasks are highly exposed to large models.

7. A village where cognitive labour is cheap: Civilization Simulator, version 13

The simulator adds a new parameter: "the price of cognitive labour". Each year, forty households have four money-related tasks: keeping and reconciling accounts, research and selection, monitoring managers, and adjusting to plan. Each task is partly cognitive labour and partly checking and bearing responsibility. A household does a task only when its benefit exceeds its cost. Drag the price of cognitive labour to see how many households, and how many tasks, become affordable; then hand checking to the scribes as well, and see what happens.

8. What this step leaves open

The price of cognitive labour has collapsed, but checking and bearing responsibility have not fallen with it, and the scribes' capabilities have an uneven edge. The earlier steps therefore have to be derived again:

  1. The boundary of delegation. Once research and comparison are almost free, can households without much wealth also afford a personal agent?
  2. Commitment and guarantees. The scribes have no property to pledge and no accumulated reputation. Who will guarantee their actions, and how can what they did be checked?
  3. Competition over information. When everyone has a scribe reading reports for them, information advantages will be worn away faster. Where will new asymmetries appear?
  4. Participants in the market. When scribes place orders directly, execution is cheaper, but will they all make the same decisions at the same time?
  5. Human bias. Can scribes help people keep to their plans, and will they also be used to exploit people's biases?
  6. What does not change. Time, risk and irreversible losses do not disappear because of the scribes.

9. Implications for products

Financial products built on large models today all stand at the start of this change in a parameter. Three implications follow. First, to judge a product's value, look at which part of the cost it lowers. If it lowers only the cost of research and drafting while the cost of checking and bearing responsibility stays the same, the improvement users get has a ceiling. Second, because the edge of capability is uneven, checking cannot be allocated by how hard a task is; it must be allocated by the consequences of an error and by how far the result can be checked. Third, the overall amplification factor depends on the slowest step; a product must redesign account opening, authorization, settlement and checking together, rather than only making one step faster.
