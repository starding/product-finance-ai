Orders, rules and market structureRules decide who bears the cost

Realized return =Time compensation＋Risk compensation＋Information advantage－Trading costs＋LuckPremise: promises kept

Trading costs depend not only on the spread but also on rules. Whether a trade is filled, and at what price, depends on a whole set of rules: order types decide how traders trade off price against certainty, priority rules decide who is filled first, and market structure decides who supplies liquidity. These rules look like technical details, but in fact they decide who bears the cost of trading.

1. Order types: trading off price against certainty

In Trading and Exchanges: Market Microstructure for Practitioners, Harris points out that choosing an order type is in essence a choice between two risks: the risk of not being filled, and the risk of being filled at a poor price.

  • Market order: filled immediately at the best opposing price in the market. It is almost certain to be filled, but the price is uncertain.
  • Limit order: filled only at the specified price or better. The price is guaranteed, but the order may never be filled.
  • Stop order: becomes a market order when the price reaches a set level. It is used to limit losses, but once triggered it may be filled at a price far worse than the set level.
  • Stop-limit order: becomes a limit order when triggered. The price is guaranteed, but if the price gaps, the order may not be filled at all.

2. Who is filled first: priority rules

Many orders often queue at the same price. Almost every exchange applies price priority first: buy orders with higher bids and sell orders with lower asks are filled first. Among orders at the same price, the most common second rule is time priority, first come, first served; some futures markets use pro-rata allocation, which allocates fills in proportion to the size of resting orders. Harris notes that priority rules decide who is willing to post orders and supply liquidity: time priority rewards those who post early, while pro-rata allocation encourages large orders.

3. Market structure: who supplies liquidity

By who supplies liquidity, markets fall into a few basic structures:

  • Quote-driven markets: market makers continuously quote bid and ask prices, and traders trade with them. Traditional bond and foreign-exchange markets are mostly of this kind.
  • Order-driven markets: traders' limit orders are gathered into an order book and matched by the exchange according to its rules. Most stock exchanges today are of this kind, and the traders who post limit orders are the suppliers of liquidity.
  • Brokered markets: brokers search for counterparties on their clients' behalf; this is common for block trades and very illiquid assets.

Most real markets are hybrids, for example with designated market makers alongside the order book. Madhavan (1992) compared the quote-driven and order-driven mechanisms.

4. Call auctions: gathering orders to trade at once

In continuous trading, orders are matched as they arrive. A call auction instead collects orders over a period and, at the set time, fills them all at once at a single price: the price that maximizes the volume traded. Exchanges around the world commonly use call auctions at the open and close. For example, the Shanghai and Shenzhen stock exchanges run an opening call auction from 9:15 to 9:25 in the morning and a closing call auction from 14:57 to 15:00. Call auctions concentrate scattered orders and reduce price jumps at the open and close, and as a result the closing price serves as the settlement benchmark for many funds and derivatives.

5. Circuit breakers and price limits

To keep prices from swinging violently over short periods because of panic or erroneous orders, many markets have mechanisms that halt trading. In China's A-share market, individual stocks on the main boards have a daily price limit of 10%; on ChiNext and the STAR Market the limit is 20%. The United States applies a "limit up–limit down" mechanism to individual stocks, halting trading for a few minutes when the price moves outside a band within a short time. For the market as a whole, there are three levels of circuit breaker: when the S&P 500 falls 7% or 13% from the previous close before 3:25 p.m. US Eastern Time, trading halts for 15 minutes; when it falls 20%, trading stops for the rest of the day, whenever this happens.

These mechanisms give the market time to calm down and replenish liquidity, at the cost of being unable to trade during the halt. For an investor who needs to exit immediately, that is itself a risk.

6. Best execution and order routing

The same stock often trades on several venues. When a broker receives a client's order, it must decide where to send it. Regulation NMS, issued by the US Securities and Exchange Commission in 2005, requires that trades not be executed at prices worse than the best prices publicly quoted on other venues. FINRA Rule 5310 requires brokers to seek "best execution" for their clients. Brokers must also disclose each quarter where they send orders and arrangements such as payment for order flow received from trading venues, so that clients can judge conflicts of interest.

7. Implications for product design

Trading rules decide whether users are filled, at what price, and when the proceeds actually arrive. Products should explain these rules clearly.
