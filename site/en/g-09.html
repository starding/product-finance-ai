Chapter 9

Prices carry everyone's informationInformation, prices and the zero-sum game

Once the market square opened, someone spotted an opportunity. The man who lived on the hill always saw the ships coming home first, so he bought ship shares before the news spread and sold them once everyone else knew. When he bought, the price moved; others saw the price moving and guessed there might be good news. Prices thus pool information scattered among many people: each person knows only a little, but the price combines what all of them know. Because the price already reflects what people know, how it changes from here depends only on what nobody knows yet, and so it is hard to predict. Market makers pay a cost for trading with the informed, gathering information is costly, and the gains from information are zero-sum among traders.

1. The man on the hill sees the ships first

One family in the village lives on the hill, and whenever a ship returns from far away, he is always the first to see it. Before the news reaches the village gate, he hurries to the market square and buys ship shares: if the ship comes home safely, the shares will rise in value. The more he buys, the more the market maker sells, and the quote rises with it. By the time the ship docks and the whole village knows, the price has already risen all the way; he sells and pockets the difference.

Today's counterpart. Trading securities on material non-public information is defined as insider trading in most markets today and is prohibited by law. Getting ahead by analysing public information faster and more carefully, however, remains a way that many traders compete.

2. Prices pool scattered information

The man on the hill is only one example. Every villager knows something others do not: the fisherman knows the recent winds, the carpenter knows the state of the ships' repairs, the merchant knows the price of salt on other islands. No one knows everything, but each person buys or sells according to what they know, and the price pulls these scraps of information together. Someone who sees the price rise does not need to know whether the cause is the wind, the repairs or the salt price elsewhere; by adjusting their own buying and selling to the price, they are in effect using what others know. Hayek (1945) called this role of prices a mechanism for communicating information: people need not know why something has become scarce; seeing its price rise, they economize on it.

A well-known example comes from Galton (1907). At a country fair, 787 people entered a contest to guess the weight of an ox after slaughter. Individual guesses were far apart, yet the median of all the guesses was within one per cent of the actual weight. The many individual errors cancelled each other out, and the pooled guess was more accurate than the great majority of the individual ones.

Today's counterpart. A company's share price combines the scattered information held separately by analysts, customers, suppliers and competitors.

3. Why prices are hard to predict

If everyone knew the ship would return tomorrow, people would rush to buy today, the price would rise all the way today, and it would not rise again tomorrow for the same reason. Whatever can be foreseen is already reflected in today's price; tomorrow's change can come only from news that nobody has today, and whether that news will be good or bad cannot be known in advance. So in a market where people anticipate correctly, price changes cannot be predicted.

Once a predictable pattern is discovered, it gets exploited, and the exploitation makes it disappear. Suppose the ships always return after the harvest festival and the shares always rise afterwards. People will then buy before the festival, the rise moves earlier, to before the festival, then earlier still, until the pattern no longer exists.

Derived so far: a principle

Efficient market hypothesis

DisciplineFinance › Asset pricingTypeHypothesis

Prices fully reflect available information; only new information moves them, and new information cannot be known in advance, so price changes cannot be predicted.

Standing in the field
The 2013 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences went to Fama, Hansen and Shiller "for their empirical analysis of asset prices"; the announcement noted that Fama and his collaborators showed stock prices are extremely hard to predict in the short run and that new information is very quickly incorporated into prices
Constraint it answers
Each person knows only part of the information and trades on what they know
Premises
Someone trades on information as soon as it appears; trading and gathering information cost nothing; people broadly agree on what a piece of information means
Proposed by
Samuelson, 1965, proved that properly anticipated prices change unpredictably; Fama, 1970, called a market whose prices fully reflect available information an efficient market, and distinguished weak, semi-strong and strong forms by the scope of the information
Open question
If prices already reflect everything, who is still willing to pay to gather information?

Today's counterpart. Positive comments about individual stocks on television are typically fully reflected in the price within about a minute (Busse and Green, 2002). McLean and Pontiff (2016) examined 97 stock-selection patterns found in academic research; after the papers were published, the returns of portfolios built on these patterns fell by an average of 58%.

4. Market makers and the informed

A market maker quotes prices to buy and sell at any time, but does not know whether the person coming to trade already knows something. Trading with the uninformed, he earns the spread; trading with the informed, he is bound to lose, because the informed buy only when the price is too low and sell only when it is too high. To stay in business, the market maker must use the spread earned from the uninformed to cover his losses to the informed, so the more informed traders there are, the wider the spread. The uninformed therefore pay a little in every trade for the existence of the informed. Glosten and Milgrom (1985) described this mechanism in a model.

Today's counterpart. Bid-ask spreads on stocks often widen before major announcements, or when the market is full of rumours.

5. Gathering information is costly

The man on the hill must keep watch every day, the fisherman must go to sea, and the merchant must travel between the islands: gathering information costs something. If prices already reflected all information, no one would be willing to pay that cost, because knowing would earn them nothing. But if no one gathered information, prices would reflect none. Neither extreme can hold, so prices cannot fully reflect all information. The market settles where the share of informed traders is just large enough that the benefit of gathering information equals its cost: prices reflect most of the information but leave a little room to compensate those who paid. When more people dig for news, prices carry more information, each person gains less from digging, and some stop; when fewer dig, prices carry less, and digging becomes worthwhile again.

Grossman–Stiglitz paradox

DisciplineEconomics › Information economics (financial markets)TypeTheoretical model

When gathering information is costly, prices cannot fully reflect all information; in equilibrium the share of informed traders is such that the benefit of gathering information just offsets its cost, and prices convey only part of what the informed know.

Standing in the field
The paper is titled "On the impossibility of informationally efficient markets", and the contradiction it exposed is commonly called the Grossman–Stiglitz paradox; Stiglitz shared the 2001 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences with Akerlof and Spence for analyses of markets with asymmetric information
Constraint it answers
Gathering information is costly
Premises
Information can be obtained only at a cost; prices reveal part of the informed traders' information to the uninformed; prices also contain disturbances unrelated to information
Proposed by
Grossman and Stiglitz, 1980
Open question
When research becomes cheaper and more people do it, how does each person's gain from research change?

Today's counterpart. Professional investment firms devote substantial resources to research; the cheaper research becomes and the more people do it, the less each of them gains from it.

6. Information advantage is zero-sum

What the informed trader earns is exactly what the people trading with him lose. Taking all traders together, before costs, whenever someone earns more, someone else earns less; after the costs of gathering information and trading, the group as a whole loses. By staying out of this guessing game and holding all the ship shares in proportion, one gets the average result of all the ships without paying these costs.

This conclusion uses nothing but arithmetic. Every ship share in the village is held either by people who hold everything in proportion or by people who actively pick. The former get exactly the average of all ship shares. The two groups together must also get the average of all ship shares, so before costs the active pickers as a group can only get the average too. They pay extra for digging up news and for trading; after those costs, as a group they must fall behind.

Derived so far: a principle

The arithmetic of active management

DisciplineFinance › Investment managementTypeTheorem

Before costs, the average return of active managers as a group equals that of passive investors who hold everything; after costs, active managers as a group must fall behind.

Standing in the field
The paper is titled "The arithmetic of active management"; its author states that the two assertions hold for any time period and depend only on the laws of addition, subtraction, multiplication and division
Constraint it answers
Information advantage is zero-sum among traders
Premises
Passive investors hold all securities in proportion to their market shares; returns are averaged weighted by the money invested; active management costs more than passive holding
Proposed by
Sharpe, 1991
Open question
Among the active pickers, who wins and who loses; and if everyone held passively, who would set prices?

Today's counterpart. Index funds hold all stocks in proportion to their market shares; they do no research and no picking, and their fees are very low. Their very existence is a response to the zero-sum nature of competition over information.

7. Informed and uninformed: Civilization Simulator, version 9

The simulator unlocks the rule "Information in prices". Each year a new piece of news about the fleet's prospects appears. Those who pay to dig for it learn it first; everyone else must wait until it becomes public. You can adjust the share of people who dig for news, the cost of digging, and how many of the uninformed still trade frequently, and see how much of the news the price reflects in advance, along with the cumulative gains and losses of three groups over twenty years.

8. What this step leaves open

Prices pool information, and they also make information advantage costly and zero-sum. New questions follow:

  1. Ordinary people have neither the time nor the expertise. Should they dig for news and judge for themselves, or hand the job to people who specialize in it?
  2. People who dig for news and trade on others' behalf may not do their duty. Whether they are really acting for the principal is hard for the principal to know.
  3. Inside information and deception. Some trade on information others cannot possibly have; some deliberately spread false news. Rules and enforcement are needed.
  4. People make their own mistakes. Even knowing that passive holding is better, people still chase rising prices, sell into falls and trade too often.

9. Implications for products

Market data, research, trading and index products are all tied to the mechanisms in this chapter. Three implications follow. First, predicting prices should not be the promise. Most information that is already public is already reflected in prices; if a product claims it can predict prices consistently, ask where its information comes from and why others do not have it. Second, competition over information is zero-sum and, after costs, negative for the group as a whole; lowering users' trading and research costs and helping them avoid unnecessary trades is value that can last. Third, the cheaper research becomes and the more people do it, the smaller the advantage research brings; this is especially worth remembering as analysis tools become widespread.
