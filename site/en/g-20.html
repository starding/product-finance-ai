Chapter 20

One person and one sum of moneyWhat a product promises

A thirty-year-old villager brings his savings to a scribe. He wants to build a new house in five years, he wants to stop working the fields after sixty, and he wants to keep some grain aside for emergencies. The three have different deadlines and can bear different ups and downs: the grain for the house must be there in five years, while the grain for retirement can wait thirty. He has a harvest every year, and his future labour is itself a sizeable part of his wealth. The scribe must first find out what he wants and how much he can bear before deciding how to arrange things. In the end it must answer one question: what can it promise him? Time, systematic risk and irreversible losses do not disappear because of the scribe; it can promise only the part it can actually deliver.

1. What he wants

This villager cannot say "I want such-and-such an annual return". What he can name are a few concrete things: a house in five years, retirement in thirty, and a little kept at hand for emergencies. Each has an amount and a deadline, and being late or short on either has a cost. The first thing a product must do is not recommend ship shares but write these things down one by one: how much is needed, when, and what happens if it comes a little late or a little short.

There is one new constraint: the user has his own goals, deadlines and capacity to bear risk, and the product must make arrangements for this one person and this one sum of money.

Today's counterpart. Financial planning usually begins by setting out the user's goals: education, buying a home, retirement and an emergency reserve, each with an amount and a deadline.

2. How much he can bear

Bearing risk has two meanings. One is capacity: how steady his harvest is, how many mouths he has to feed, how long until each goal falls due. The steadier the harvest and the longer the horizon, the larger the swings he can bear. The other is willingness: faced with the same swings, some people sleep soundly and some do not. With little capacity but high willingness, he will take risks he should not; with ample capacity but low willingness, he will miss compensation he could have earned. The scribe must ask about the two separately and go by whichever is lower.

Today's counterpart. The suitability rule of the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority in the United States lists what a broker must learn about a customer before making a recommendation, including age, other investments, financial situation and needs, tax status, investment objectives, investment experience, investment time horizon, liquidity needs and risk tolerance.

3. How much risk to take

Suppose there are only two places to put grain: the granary, with almost no risk and a very low return, and ship shares, with a higher return and larger swings. Each extra portion in ship shares brings more expected return and more swings. The more this villager dislikes swings, or the larger the swings of ship shares, the lower the share he puts in ship shares; the higher the extra return of ship shares, the higher that share. Followed through to the end, the share depends only on these three things, and not on how much grain he has or how old he is.

Derived so far: a principle

Merton's portfolio choice

DisciplineFinance › Portfolio theory (intertemporal portfolio choice)TypeTheoretical model

When the returns on risky assets are unpredictable and the investor's degree of risk aversion does not change with wealth, among other conditions, the optimal share an investor puts in risky assets equals the risky asset's return in excess of the risk-free return, divided by the product of the risk-aversion coefficient and the square of the risky asset's volatility; this share does not change with the investment horizon or the amount of wealth.

Standing in the field
Together with Samuelson's discrete-time paper, published the same year in the same journal, it established in 1969 the basic result of lifetime portfolio selection: under these conditions, the investment horizon does not affect portfolio choice; the share later came to be commonly called the "Merton ratio"
Constraint it answers
Future returns are uncertain, and people are averse to risk
Premises
Returns on risky assets are unpredictable; the degree of risk aversion does not change with wealth; the portfolio can be adjusted at any time; there are no other assets such as labour income
Proposed by
Merton, 1969; Samuelson, 1969
Open question
Besides savings, people have future labour income; does it count as part of the portfolio?

Today's counterpart. The result of a risk-assessment questionnaire usually corresponds to a range for the split between stocks and bonds.

4. A person is an asset too

The previous step left something out: this villager's harvests over the next thirty years. As long as his harvest is steady, it is like a bond that pays interest every year: predictable, and far larger than his current savings. Count it as part of his wealth, and he in fact holds a great deal of "safe assets", so his savings can bear more swings. As he grows older, fewer future harvests remain, and the share of his savings bearing swings should fall accordingly. If he can also work a few more years and stop later, he has room to manoeuvre in bad years and can bear even more. Conversely, if his harvest itself rises and falls with the fleet, for example because he works on the ships, his future income is less like a bond and more like ship shares, and his savings should be kept safer.

Derived so far: a principle

Human capital and life-cycle portfolios

DisciplineFinance › Portfolio theory (life-cycle financial planning)TypeTheoretical model

Future labour income is an asset and should be counted in the portfolio together with financial wealth. The steadier the labour income and the more room there is to adjust how much one works and when one retires, the more risk financial wealth can bear, and the risk taken when young can be higher than when old.

Standing in the field
The paper treats labour supply, consumption and investment as joint choices over a lifetime, shows that flexibility in work and retirement timing allows people to bear more investment risk, and uses this to explain why the young can hold riskier portfolios
Constraint it answers
A person's lifetime income is concentrated in the working years
Premises
The risk of labour income is not highly correlated with the risk of risky assets; people can adjust how much they work and when they retire
Proposed by
Bodie, Merton and Samuelson, 1992
Open question
How should someone whose labour income rises and falls with the fleet arrange things?

Today's counterpart. Target-date funds gradually lower their share of stocks as the target date approaches.

5. One account for each goal

Combining the three goals and computing a single share creates a dilemma: set by the retirement horizon, the house money takes too much risk; set by the house horizon, the retirement money is too conservative. The more natural approach is the one people already use in their heads: keep a separate account for each goal. The emergency grain goes in the granary, where it can be drawn at any time. The house grain is needed in five years, so most of it goes somewhere safe. The retirement grain has thirty years, so more of it can go into ship shares. The risk of each account is measured by the standard this goal cares about most: how likely it is to fall far short when it comes due.

Derived so far: a principle

Goal-based mental accounts

DisciplineFinance › Behavioural finance (portfolio choice)TypeTheoretical model

Wealth is divided by goal into several mental accounts, and each account chooses its portfolio according to its own horizon and threshold, with risk defined as the probability that the account falls short of its threshold when it comes due; under certain conditions, the aggregate portfolio formed by combining the accounts is still mean–variance efficient.

Standing in the field
The paper combines Markowitz's mean–variance theory with Shefrin and Statman's behavioural portfolio theory, and later literature treats it as one of the foundations of goal-based wealth management
Constraint it answers
A person has several goals with different horizons and thresholds
Premises
The amount, horizon and threshold of each goal can be stated clearly; necessary adjustments between accounts are allowed
Proposed by
Das, Markowitz, Scheid and Statman, 2010; the concept of mental accounting comes from Thaler, 1985
Open question
Who maintains the boundaries between accounts, and can money be moved across them in an emergency?

Today's counterpart. Some retirement accounts and education savings accounts receive separate tax treatment, with their uses and withdrawal conditions also set separately.

6. What can be promised

At this point it is possible to say what the scribe can promise him. It cannot promise that the grain for the house will certainly be enough, or that the grain for retirement will certainly be plentiful: time will not grow shorter, and storms that strike the whole village will still come. What it can promise is the part it actually controls: to hold the agreed allocation, to deposit and rebalance on time, to keep fees within the agreed ceiling and the risk of each account within the agreed limit, and to tell him truthfully how likely each goal is to be met and what the worst run of years would look like.

Such promises can be checked. The agreed allocation is written down, every step of execution is recorded, and the limits on fees and risk can be compared item by item. Whether the result is good depends on the run of years, but not on whether the scribe kept its word. What is promised is a process; what is delivered is a distribution of outcomes.

Today's counterpart. Asset management products usually promise an investment scope, investment proportions, fee rates and information disclosure, but not returns.

7. One household's agreement: Civilization Simulator, version 20

From this version on, the simulator looks at a single villager. You can set his age, whether his harvest is steady, how strongly he dislikes swings, and how much he can save each year, then see the allocation the scribe gives each of the three accounts and, across two thousand runs of years, how likely each goal is to be met on time.

8. What this step leaves open

Once the product knows what to promise, new questions follow:

  1. He cannot always be present. Once the agreement is set, the scribe must keep acting on his behalf. How detailed must the agreement be, and how much authority should it grant?
  2. He cannot see the process. How can he understand and check whether the scribe acts according to the agreement?
  3. Agreements go out of date. His harvest, family and goals will change. Who updates the agreement, and when?
  4. Agents make mistakes. If the limits of the promise are breached, who bears the consequences?

9. Implications for products

The first part an agent-centred wealth product must grow is "what to promise". Three implications follow. First, start from the user's goals, not from the product: first write down how much is needed, when, and what happens if it comes late or short, then decide how to arrange things. Second, assess capacity and willingness to bear risk separately and go by the lower; count future labour income as part of the user's wealth, so that the portfolio changes with age and occupation. Third, promise the process, not the outcome: the allocation, execution, fees and risk limits are all written clearly and can be checked, and the user is told truthfully how likely each goal is to be met.
