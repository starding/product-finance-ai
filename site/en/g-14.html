Chapter 14

Can everyone have a personal agent?How the boundary of delegation moves

In the past, only the few households with the most property could afford a dedicated steward. A steward had to learn each household's harvest, debts and intentions, and then choose ships, keep the accounts and make adjustments on schedule for it. This labour had to be done afresh for every household, and households with little property could not pay for it. Most people could only buy a standardized arrangement, such as simply holding a proportional share of all the ship shares. Once the scribes arrived, this labour cost almost nothing, the threshold fell, and standardized arrangements could become arrangements made for each household. Yet the cost of checking and bearing responsibility remains, and the poorest households are still shut out; of the agency costs, only monitoring falls clearly; and an agent that charges its principal nothing will sooner or later work for whoever does pay it.

1. Stewards used to belong only to the wealthy

On the island, the efficiency of farming, weaving and shipbuilding rises year after year, and grain and cloth grow ever cheaper. A steward's work, however, has not become any faster: getting to know a household, and choosing and checking on its behalf, takes as much time now as it did a hundred years ago. As wages rise in other trades, nobody would be willing to work as a steward unless stewards' pay rose too; so stewards' pay rises as well, and hiring a steward becomes ever more expensive relative to buying a bolt of cloth. The steward has not got worse; other things have simply become cheaper.

Derived so far: a principle

Cost disease

DisciplineEconomics › Economic growth and service-sector economicsTypeTheoretical model (two-sector model)

When productivity in one trade stagnates while productivity in other trades keeps rising, wages in the first trade must still rise with the general level, so its relative price keeps climbing.

Standing in the field
Named after its proposer and commonly called "Baumol's cost disease"; the original used it to explain the fiscal difficulties of urban public services, and it has since often been used to explain the long-run rise in the prices of services such as education and health care
Constraint it answers
One-to-one services must be provided household by household, and the labour cannot be saved
Premises
The labour a service requires does not fall with technological progress; wages in different trades follow one another
Proposed by
Baumol, 1967, using education, health care and live performance as examples
Open question
Can the labour of checking and bearing responsibility also become cheaper?
After the rerun
The scribes make the productivity of the cognitive labour in a steward's work leap upward, so that part no longer meets the premise of "stagnant productivity"; the part that is checking and bearing responsibility still meets it

In rerunning the step of delegation, only one thing changes: the scribes make the cognitive labour in a steward's work almost free.

Today's counterpart. One-to-one wealth management services usually set a high minimum investment and mainly serve clients with substantial assets.

2. The threshold: a fixed cost divided by wealth

A large part of a steward's cost does not depend on how much property a household has. Whether a household has a hundred portions or a thousand, learning its circumstances and keeping and checking its accounts take about the same labour. Divide this fixed cost by the household's property and you get the cost of delegation as a share of its property; the less property, the higher the share. Delegation pays only when this share is lower than the benefit delegation brings. Every household compares three approaches: managing on its own, buying a standardized arrangement, or hiring a personal steward; it chooses whichever has the lowest total cost. Once the cognitive labour within the fixed cost becomes cheaper, the cost of a personal steward falls, and households that could previously only manage on their own or buy a standardized product can now afford a personal arrangement.

Transaction costs and the boundary of the firm

DisciplineEconomics › New institutional economics (transaction cost economics)TypeTheory

Whether a task is done oneself, handed to the market or handed to an organization depends on the relative costs of the different approaches; when the cost of one approach falls, the boundary moves towards it.

Standing in the field
The paper posed the question "why do firms exist" and answered it with the cost of using the market; Coase received the 1991 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences "for his discovery and clarification of the significance of transaction costs and property rights for the institutional structure and functioning of the economy"
Constraint it answers
Attention and expertise are scarce, and delegation itself has a cost
Premises
The costs of the different approaches can be compared; part of the cost does not depend on scale
Proposed by
Coase, 1937
Open question
If the cost of checking and bearing responsibility does not fall, how can the households with the least property get delegation?
After the rerun
The cognitive-labour part of the fixed cost of delegation almost disappears, and the boundary moves towards personal delegation; the part that is checking and bearing responsibility stays the same and determines where the boundary stops moving

Reher and Sokolinski (2024) studied a large US robo-adviser that cut its minimum investment from US$5,000 to US$500. Participation by middle-income investors rose clearly, but participation by the poorest investors did not. The threshold came down, but not far enough for everyone to clear it.

Today's counterpart. Robo-advisers offer portfolios allocated by questionnaire and rebalanced automatically, with relatively low minimum investments and fees.

3. From standardized to personal

Households that cannot afford a steward can only accept arrangements designed for the majority: one portfolio, one default proportion, one path that adjusts with age. These arrangements suit the average household but do not necessarily suit any particular one. If someone in the family has an unstable income, or the household will need a large sum in a few years, the right arrangement differs from the average one. The scribes can work things out for each household separately, so standardized arrangements can become personal ones.

D'Acunto, Prabhala and Rossi (2019) studied a portfolio optimization tool introduced by a large Indian brokerage. Investors who were under-diversified before using it came to hold more stocks, and their portfolios became less volatile and earned better returns. Investors who were already well diversified came to hold fewer stocks and traded more often. Users' biases, such as the disposition effect, trend chasing and the rank effect, also declined.

Today's counterpart. Target-date funds are tiered by age; questionnaire-based portfolios are tiered by risk level; finer-grained personal allocation used to be found only in private banking.

4. Which agency cost falls

What a principal pays for delegation can be divided into three components: the monitoring cost of keeping watch on the manager; the bonding cost the manager incurs to make the principal trust them; and the residual loss caused by diverging interests that remains after monitoring and bonding. The scribes affect the three differently. The cost of monitoring falls clearly: a principal can have their own scribe check, transaction by transaction, what the manager did. The cost of bonding hardly changes: a scribe has no property to pledge, and whoever guarantees it is still the person behind it. The residual loss depends on whom the scribe works for.

Rerun: a principle re-examined

The three components of agency costs

DisciplineFinance › Corporate finance (agency theory)TypeTheory

Agency costs consist of three components, the principal's monitoring costs, the agent's bonding costs and the residual loss, and the principal chooses monitoring and bonding so as to minimize their sum.

Standing in the field
Framed the relationship between shareholders and managers as an agency relationship and gave a definition of agency costs; contract theory, which studies incentives and contracts, earned Hart and Holmström the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 2016
Constraint it answers
The agent's interests differ from the principal's, and the agent's actions cannot be fully observed
Premises
Both monitoring and bonding consume resources; interests cannot be fully aligned
Proposed by
Jensen and Meckling, 1976
Open question
Who guarantees an agent that has no property to pledge?
After the rerun
Monitoring costs fall clearly with the price of cognitive labour; bonding costs stay the same; the residual loss depends on whom the scribe works for, and may fall or may rise

Today's counterpart. Tools for reconciliation, fee comparison and holdings analysis have greatly lowered the cost of checking on fiduciaries; liability for compensation and licensing obligations still rest with the institutions.

5. It works for whoever pays it

An agent that charges its principal nothing must be paid from somewhere else. If its pay comes from those selling ship shares, as a commission on each sale, it has reason to recommend the ship shares with the highest commission rather than those best suited to the principal. Sellers then compete on this basis too: not over whose ship shares are better, but over who pays the recommender a higher commission. The principal pays nothing directly, but pays more through higher fees.

Commissions and biased advice

DisciplineEconomics › Industrial organization and financial intermediationTypeTheoretical model

When an adviser's pay comes from the seller, recommendations respond to the incentives the seller offers, and sellers turn to competing through commissions; rules such as mandatory disclosure and caps on commissions sometimes have unintended consequences.

Standing in the field
Analyses intermediaries' commissions in retail finance and medical services within a framework of asymmetric information, and evaluates the effects of common policies such as mandatory disclosure and commission caps

Premises
Principals cannot see clearly which products are good or bad; intermediaries are paid by sellers
Proposed by
Inderst and Ottaviani, 2012, using retail financial products and medical services as examples

How can an agent be paid by the principal without the threshold rising again?
After the rerun
The scribes make a personal agent cheap, but they do not change this: who pays the agent determines whose interests it considers first

Today's counterpart. Rules published by the UK Financial Services Authority in 2010 provide that from 31 December 2012 investment advisers may no longer receive commissions paid by product providers for recommending their products; advisers set their own charges and agree them with clients.

6. A village where everyone has a steward: Civilization Simulator, version 14

The simulator unlocks "Personal agent". Forty households can manage on their own, simply hold a proportional share of everything, or hire a personal agent. A personal agent charges a fixed annual fee plus a percentage fee. The cognitive-labour part falls with the price of cognitive labour, while the part for checking and bearing responsibility stays the same. It can tailor arrangements to each household and helps people chase rises and sell into falls less often. Drag the price of cognitive labour to see what each household chooses and what property each has after twenty years; then have the agent paid by sellers instead, and see what happens.

7. What this step leaves open

Personal agents have become cheap, but several questions remain unresolved:

  1. Guarantees. The scribes have no property to pledge and no accumulated reputation. When one does something wrong, who pays compensation?
  2. Checking. A principal can have their own scribe check on the manager, but who checks the principal's own scribe?
  3. The poorest households. The fixed cost of checking and bearing responsibility remains, and the households with the least property still cannot clear the threshold.
  4. When everyone has an agent. When every household has a scribe reading reports and choosing ship shares for it, how will information advantages change?

8. Implications for products

Robo-advisers and wealth assistants built on large models are both pushing the boundary of delegation. Three implications follow. First, the threshold depends on the part of the fixed cost that cannot be brought down. To make delegation affordable for more people, the cost of checking and bearing responsibility must be lowered too, not only the cost of research and advice. Second, the value of a personal arrangement lies in fitting each household, not in looking cleverer; a product should state clearly what it has done for this household that differs from a standardized arrangement. Third, how an agent is paid determines whose side it is on. An agent that charges users directly, with transparent fees, deserves more trust than one that appears to be free.
