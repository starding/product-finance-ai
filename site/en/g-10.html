Chapter 10

Ordinary people have neither the time nor the expertiseDelegation and agency

As the ships multiplied and the news grew more complicated, people who farmed had neither the time nor the expertise to judge which ship to buy. Handing their ship shares to someone who knew the trade became the natural choice: he studied shipping full time, and with many households' money pooled together, the cost of research was spread across them. Delegation saved time, and it also brought new problems. Whether the manager was any good could not be told from a few years of results; whether he was trying, the principal could not see; giving him a share of the profits made him favour risk; and even if he really was skilled, the benefit mostly went to him through the size of the money he managed and the fees he charged. The cheapest delegation was to ask someone simply to hold all the ship shares in proportion, without picking and without research.

1. Farmers have no time to study shipping

A household has to work in the fields every day. To judge which ship is worth buying, one must find out about the captain's character, the risks of the route and conditions on other islands, and keep watching prices in the market square. All this takes a great deal of time, and doing it well takes experience. For a farmer, spending that time on farming is usually a better deal than studying shipping. In The Wealth of Nations, Smith (1776) saw the division of labour as the main source of growing wealth: each person concentrates on what they do best, and then they trade with one another.

Today's counterpart. Most people have neither the time to study company accounts and industry changes nor the training to do so.

2. Handing money to someone who knows the trade

One villager knows shipping well. He proposes that each household hand him its ship shares to manage together; he will distribute the returns according to each household's share and charge a small fee each year. Researching a ship is costly for a single household, but he needs to research it only once and can apply the result to all his principals' money; the more households delegate to him, the lower the research cost per household. This is the embryo of an investment fund.

The essence of delegation is handing the right to act to someone else: the manager decides for the principals which ship to buy and when to sell. The manager's aims do not fully coincide with the principals', and the principals pay a price for this, which can be divided into three components. The first is the cost of keeping watch on the manager: checking his books and finding out what he has bought. The second is what the manager spends to reassure the principals: publishing his accounts regularly and staking part of his own wealth alongside theirs. The third is the loss that remains because their interests still diverge after both have been done. More monitoring or bonding is not always better: if an extra unit of monitoring saves less than a unit of loss, it is not worth it. What the principal wants is the smallest total of the three.

The three components of agency costs

DisciplineFinance › Corporate finance (agency theory)TypeTheory

Agency costs consist of three components: the principal's monitoring costs, the agent's bonding costs and the residual loss; the principal chooses monitoring and bonding so that the sum of the three is smallest.

Standing in the field
Framed the relationship between shareholders and managers as an agency relationship and defined agency costs; contract theory, which studies incentives and contracts, earned Hart and Holmström the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 2016
Constraint it answers
The agent's interests differ from the principal's, and the agent's actions cannot be fully seen
Premises
Monitoring and bonding both consume resources; interests cannot be made fully aligned
Proposed by
Jensen and Meckling, 1976
Open question
Can the principal design a pay scheme under which the manager chooses of his own accord to act in the principal's interest?

Earlier, Ross (1973) had posed, as a general economic problem, how a principal should design pay so that an agent acts in the principal's interest.

Today's counterpart. A fund pools many investors' money, which a fund manager manages as a whole; returns are distributed by share, and a management fee is charged.

3. Choosing the wrong person: ability is hard to see

More than one villager is willing to manage shares for others. Whom should one choose? The most direct way is to look at past results. But a ship's success depends heavily on luck, and a few years of results say little: even if no manager had any skill, luck alone would always put a few of them at the top. Telling reliably from results that a manager is genuinely skilled often takes more years than his whole career.

Fama and French (2010) used simulations to test the performance of actively managed US funds and found that, after fees, very few funds consistently beat their benchmark in a way that luck could not explain. Other researchers argue that this method lacks statistical power and may underestimate the ability of a small number of funds (Harvey and Liu, 2022).

Today's counterpart. The funds at the top of the rankings are often replaced by a different set a few years later.

4. Trying or not: invisible effort

Once a manager is chosen, there is another question: is he trying? He can go to sea every day to gather news, or sit at home and place orders carelessly. The principal sees only the result, not the effort, and the result is mixed with luck. If he receives a fixed fee each year, his income is the same whether he tries or not, and he has little reason to try. If his pay is tied to results, he has a reason, but he also bears luck risk that should not be his.

The dilemma arises because the principal sees too little. If the principal could also see other things, such as whether the manager goes to sea each day, or how other ships fared in the same year, luck and effort could be separated to some extent: if every ship had a poor harvest that year, the manager's poor result is not entirely his fault. Any information that reveals a little more about effort is worth writing into the pay agreement: pay becomes less entangled with luck while the incentive does not weaken. The more the principal can see, the cheaper it is to keep the agent in line.

The informativeness principle

DisciplineEconomics › Contract theory (principal–agent)TypeTheoretical model

Any information that reveals the agent's actions, even partially, improves the contract when written into the pay agreement; the more the principal sees, the cheaper it is to give the agent incentives.

Standing in the field
Holmström and Hart received the 2016 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences "for their contributions to contract theory"; MIT's report on the prize listed the informativeness principle introduced in this paper among his contributions to the principal–agent problem
Constraint it answers
The principal cannot see the agent's effort, and results are mixed with luck
Premises
The agent is more averse to bearing risk than the principal; the additional information is related to the agent's actions and is not mere noise
Proposed by
Holmström, 1979, in a paper titled "Moral hazard and observability"
Open question
Who provides the information that can be seen, and can it be trusted?

Today's counterpart. Fund managers' pay usually includes a part tied to performance; funds must also disclose their holdings and trades regularly, letting investors see more.

5. Profit-sharing encourages risk-taking

Suppose the agreement gives the manager 20% of the gains while the principals bear the losses themselves. The manager is then in the position of holding an option that can only win: the more volatile the outcome, the more he expects to receive, and the more risk the principals bear. The manager therefore has reason to bet on riskier ships.

The remedies follow from this: a performance fee is charged only once net value exceeds its previous high, and the manager must invest some of his own money, sharing the principals' fortunes.

Today's counterpart. The "high-water mark" common in private funds, and requirements that fund managers invest in the products they manage, are both responses to this problem.

6. Who gets the benefit of skill

Suppose there really is a manager in the village more skilled than the others. Word spreads, and more and more people hand him their ship shares. But only a few ships are worth betting on, and the more money he manages, the harder it is for him to find equally good opportunities; his margin above the average shrinks as he grows. As long as handing shares to him is still a better deal than handing them to others, people keep moving their shares over, until, after fees, giving them to him earns no more than giving them to someone else. At that point his skill has not disappeared; its benefit has simply turned into the size of the money he manages and the fees he collects. Even if most managers are genuinely skilled, what principals get after fees is a return comparable to passive holding. People who move their money to chase performance are not foolish; it is precisely their inflows that keep performance from persisting.

Derived so far: a principle

Money chases performance; performance does not persist

DisciplineFinance › Investment management (mutual funds)TypeTheoretical model

When active management has decreasing returns to scale and principals compete to hand their money to good managers, money flows to the managers who perform well until their excess return after fees disappears; the benefit of skill goes to the manager through size and fees, so past performance does not predict future performance.

Standing in the field
The paper is titled "Mutual fund flows and performance in rational markets"; it won the fifth Stephen A. Ross Prize in Financial Economics, whose committee said it redefined the questions that mutual fund research asks
Constraint it answers
Principals cannot tell how skilled a manager is and can only infer it from performance
Premises
Active management has decreasing returns to scale; principals rationally update their judgement of a manager's skill from past performance; the supply of capital is competitive
Proposed by
Berk and Green, 2004
Open question
If principals' judgements of managers' skill are systematically optimistic, where will the money flow?

The model does not assume that managers lack skill. By the authors' calibration, most managers are skilled enough at least to earn back their own fees.

Today's counterpart. Funds with outstanding performance often grow rapidly in size, and their later performance often reverts to the ordinary.

7. The cheapest delegation: hold everything in proportion

Since picking a manager is hard, and picking itself rarely beats the average, there is a simpler form of delegation: ask someone simply to hold all ship shares in proportion to each ship's share, with no research, no picking and no market timing, for a very low fee. The principals then get the average return of the whole village fleet, and save the cost of research and the risk of picking. The difference in fees looks small, but it compounds year after year: a gap of a little over one percentage point a year adds up to a large sum over several decades.

In 1976, the first index fund for ordinary investors was launched. Many people saw it as a joke at the time; it later became the default choice of millions of people.

Today's counterpart. Index funds and other products that hold all stocks in proportion to market value usually charge far lower fees than actively managed funds.

8. Whom to hand it to: Civilization Simulator, version 10

The simulator unlocks the rule "Delegation". Forty households can manage their ship shares themselves, hand them to a manager who picks actively, or hand them to a manager who simply holds everything in proportion. You can adjust the share of managers who are genuinely skilled, the fee for active management, and whether principals switch to whichever manager did best last year, and see each household's wealth after twenty years and the time spent each year.

9. What this step leaves open

Delegation lets ordinary people take part in complex investment too, but new questions follow:

  1. The manager may misappropriate the principals' assets. He holds everyone's ship shares; how can the principals confirm they are still there?
  2. Sellers know more. A manager knows far more about a product's risks than the principal. He may talk only about returns and not about risks, or recommend unsuitable products to people they do not suit.
  3. Principals make their own mistakes. Switching managers to chase last year's performance and redeeming during falls often leave principals with lower returns than the funds themselves earned.
  4. Delegation itself is expensive. Research, monitoring and fees all cost money; if these costs fell sharply, how would the boundary of delegation shift?

10. Implications for products

Funds, investment advisers and all kinds of products that manage money on clients' behalf are built on the mechanisms in this chapter. Three implications follow. First, the value of delegation comes from the time saved and the research cost shared, and its price is the agency cost; a delegation product should make clear what principals pay and what they get. Second, past performance is mixed with luck, and recommending managers by short-term rankings is unreliable; the more of the process the principal can see, the more credible the delegation. Third, the cheapest delegation is often also the hardest to beat; the higher a delegation product's fees, the more it needs to explain what the extra payment buys.
