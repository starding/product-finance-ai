Chapter 12

The institutions are in place, and people still make mistakesBias, inertia and defaults

Saving, lending, insurance, banks, shares, markets and delegation are all in place, and custody, disclosure and suitability have their rules. Yet people themselves still make mistakes. In spring a household resolves to store half the harvest, and when autumn comes it eats a good deal more than planned. Seeing which ship earned the most last year, people switch all their ship shares into it. After a storm they rush to sell their ship shares cheaply. People weight the present too heavily, and they have inertia: when they do not want to make a decision, they do nothing. Inertia can be turned to their advantage. Make a good arrangement the default that happens when nothing is done; let people commit now and increase later; let purchases happen automatically on schedule. The architecture of choice can also be used against people. What matters is who sets the default, and for whom.

1. Planned saving abandoned halfway

At sowing time in spring, a household works it out clearly: after this autumn's harvest it will store half, for its own old age. But once the harvest is in and the granary is full, a good meal or a new piece of clothing seems more pressing than old age decades away, and what gets stored is usually far less than planned. Next spring the household makes the same plan, and in autumn the plan fails in the same way.

The new constraint is this: people do not always act on their own plans.

The problem is that people do not weigh the present against the future consistently over time. Ask the household: would you rather eat 1 extra portion of grain next autumn, or 1.5 extra portions the autumn after? It is willing to wait the extra year. But when next autumn arrives, the same question becomes 1 extra portion today or 1.5 extra portions next year, and it no longer wants to wait. The plan it makes today for next year is one it will not want to carry out when next year comes. If it knows it will behave this way, it will be willing to lock itself in beforehand, for example by storing grain in a granary that is hard to draw from.

Derived so far: a principle

Quasi-hyperbolic discounting

DisciplineEconomics › Behavioural economics (intertemporal choice)TypeTheoretical model

People are especially impatient about the immediate future and comparatively patient about the more distant future; so plans made today for the future are ones they will not want to carry out when the future arrives, which gives them a motive to restrict their own choices in advance.

Standing in the field
Commonly called "quasi-hyperbolic discounting" or the "β–δ model"; the paper uses it to show that people with time-inconsistent preferences have a motive to constrain their future selves, which makes illiquid assets a commitment device
Constraint it answers
People do not always act on their own plans
Premises
The trade-off between present and future is inconsistent across points in time; people know, at least in part, that their future selves will behave this way
Proposed by
Laibson, 1997; this form of discounting was first used by Phelps and Pollak (1968) for intergenerational saving, and Laibson applied it to the choices of one person at different points in time
Open question
If people are willing to lock themselves in, who provides a suitable lock, and how can it be kept from locking too tightly?

Today's counterpart. Many people resolve every year to save more, yet find little left at the end of each month.

2. Inertia: doing nothing is also a choice

Another common tendency is inertia. Joining a savings plan means filling in a form, choosing a rate and choosing which ship shares to buy. When people do not want to make these decisions, they do nothing, putting it off to next month and then to next year. As a result, whether people save depends to a large extent on what happens when they do nothing.

Madrian and Shea (2001) studied a US company that changed its retirement savings plan from "opt in voluntarily" to "enrolled automatically, free to opt out". Participation among new employees rose sharply. Most also stayed at the default contribution rate and default fund the company had set, and few adjusted them.

Today's counterpart. Many countries have moved their workplace pension plans to automatic enrolment: employees who make no choice are enrolled by default.

3. The two sides of defaults

Inertia makes defaults especially powerful: whatever is set, most people stay with it. This can help people, and it can also harm them. If the default contribution rate is too low, many people save too little for good. If the default investment is too conservative, many people earn very low returns for decades. A default is not neutral; whoever sets it is in effect deciding for most people.

Defaults are not the only thing at work. For the same set of options, which one is listed first, which one is already selected, and which step requires the person to fill something in all change what people end up choosing, and no arrangement is entirely neutral. Since someone has to decide on the arrangement anyway, it can be arranged deliberately in people's favour, while leaving everyone free to choose otherwise.

Derived so far: a principle

Choice architecture

DisciplineEconomics › Behavioural economics (behavioural public policy)TypeTheory

How options are presented, including their order, the default and the steps required, systematically affects what people choose; since no presentation is neutral, people can be steered, without forbidding any option or appreciably changing incentives, towards the option they would choose after thinking it through, a design known as a nudge.

Standing in the field
The 2017 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences was awarded to Thaler "for his contributions to behavioural economics"; the prize announcement noted that he coined the term nudge and that nudges can help people exercise better self-control in settings such as saving for retirement
Constraint it answers
People do not always act on their own plans
Premises
People have inertia and limited attention; the cost of choosing otherwise is low enough
Proposed by
Thaler and Sunstein, 2008
Open question
Who designs the architecture of choice, and how can it be made to serve the person choosing?

Today's counterpart. Automatic-enrolment retirement plans usually also set a default contribution rate and a default investment, such as a target-date fund that adjusts the stock–bond mix automatically with age.

4. Commit now, increase later

Since people weight the present too heavily and have inertia, both tendencies can be put to work: commit now to raising the savings rate step by step out of future pay rises. The commitment is made today, but nobody has to eat less today. At each future pay rise the savings rate goes up automatically, and those who do not want to change anything need do nothing. Thaler and Benartzi (2004) introduced this "Save More Tomorrow" plan at one company. Participants' average savings rate rose from 3.5% to 13.6% within 40 months, and most stayed in the plan. A research review by the US Department of Labor noted that the study did not randomly assign participants, that the decision to join may itself reflect a willingness to save, and that the causal evidence is therefore limited.

Today's counterpart. Some retirement plans offer an option to raise the contribution rate automatically each year; regular fixed-amount investment plans make purchases happen automatically on schedule.

5. Chasing gains and selling into losses: getting less than the product itself

Bias also shows up in buying and selling. Whichever ship earned the most last year, people pile into it, often buying near the top. After a storm, when ship shares fall sharply, people rush to sell, often near the bottom. The return on the ship shares themselves is one thing; the return principals actually receive, given when they bought and sold, is another. Dichev (2007) compared returns that take the timing of money flowing in and out into account with buy-and-hold returns, and found that on the New York and American stock exchanges from 1926 to 2002 the former were about 1.3 percentage points a year lower. Keswani and Stolin (2008) argue that this result is not robust: over the last two-thirds of the sample, the sign of the difference for the New York and American exchanges even reverses.

Today's counterpart. Fund investors buy heavily after good performance and redeem heavily after declines, so the return investors as a whole receive is often lower than the return of the fund itself.

6. The architecture of choice can help people or harm them

Defaults, ordering and the steps required can help people keep to their plans, and sellers can also use them for profit: pre-ticking an add-on service nobody needs, hiding the cancel button deep in a menu, putting high-fee products at the top of the list. The power of a default comes from people's inertia, so who sets it and for whom matters more than the default itself. A default set for the principal should be the one most people would choose after thinking it through. A default set for the seller turns inertia into the seller's revenue.

Today's counterpart. The EU Consumer Rights Directive requires the consumer's express consent before any extra payment beyond the price of the main contract. If a trader infers consent through a default option, meaning the consumer must untick a box to avoid the payment, the consumer is entitled to have that payment refunded.

7. Keeping to the plan: Civilization Simulator, version 12

The simulator unlocks the last rule of Part One: "Defaults". Forty households must save for old age thirty years from now. You can turn automatic saving, automatic regular investment and a cooling-off period during declines on or off, and adjust how strongly people weight the present and how readily they sell in panic. Then see how much each household has saved after thirty years, and how far the return principals actually receive falls short of the return on the ship shares themselves.

8. What this step leaves open

Starting from one person's harvest in one year, today's financial system has grown step by step. Looking back, each institution carries a cost:

  1. The cost of delegation. Research, monitoring, checking and fees all require someone's time and attention, so ordinary people can only hand their money to a small number of professional institutions.
  2. The cost of checking. Custody, disclosure and audit all exist to make the invisible visible, and seeing clearly itself takes labour.
  3. The cost of information. Gathering news and analysing fleets take time, so information advantages are worn away by competition.
  4. The cost of keeping to plans. Defaults and commitment devices help people overcome bias, but someone has to design and run them, and they can also be used against people.

A large part of these costs is the cost of paying someone to think and act on your behalf. If this cost fell sharply, which of these institutions would change, and which would not?

9. Implications for products

Regular investment plans, automatic-enrolment retirement plans and target-date funds all rest on the mechanisms in this chapter. Three implications follow. First, a product's value lies not only in the returns it can deliver but also in how far it helps users keep to their own plans; the return users actually receive is the test of a product. Second, the default is the most powerful design element in a product. It should be set to the option most users would choose after thinking it through, and changing it should be easy enough. Third, the architecture of choice can help people or harm them; any design that relies on inertia should be asked whose interest it serves.
