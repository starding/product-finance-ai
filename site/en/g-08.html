Chapter 8

Securities must be saleableMarkets, auctions and market making

Shares let many people invest together in a ship, but an investor may urgently need grain at any time. To sell a share, they must first find someone willing to buy, then agree on a price. Asking house by house takes time and effort, and the more urgent the need, the lower the price they must accept. When people agree to gather in the same place at set times, buyers and sellers find each other easily, and the more people come, the easier it gets. Put everyone's quotes together and you can find the price at which the most trades take place. When someone stands ready to buy and sell at any time, sellers need not wait; the cost is the spread between the buying and selling prices. Liquidity thus acquires a price: the harder something is to sell, the higher the return buyers demand.

1. Wanting to sell a share and finding no buyer

A household in the village holds a share in a ship. The ship's prospects are good, but this year someone in the family has fallen ill, and the household urgently needs grain and has to sell its share. It goes from door to door: some households have no spare grain, some do not want a share in a ship, some offer too little. One house after another, several days pass and the share is still unsold. The more urgent the need, the lower the price the household has to accept.

Shares solve the problem of financing large projects, but they bring a new one: whether a share can be sold in time and at a reasonable price.

Today's counterpart. Equity in private companies, unlisted debt and property often take a long time to sell, and a quick sale requires a steep discount.

2. A fixed place: the market square

Suppose the village agrees that on the first day of each month, everyone who wants to buy or sell shares comes to the big tree at the edge of the village. Things change. Sellers no longer need to go from door to door, and buyers know where to look. The more people come, the easier it is to find a counterparty willing to trade at a reasonable price; and the more people come, the more reason there is to come. Trading therefore gathers in a few central places.

After the Dutch East India Company issued its shares in 1602, merchants in Amsterdam began trading them within days, and a custom of trading shares at a fixed place and fixed times gradually took shape (Petram, 2014).

Today's counterpart. A stock exchange brings buyers and sellers from across a country, and even across the world, into the same market; trading in any one stock tends to be concentrated on one or two venues.

3. How the price is set: the call auction

Once everyone has arrived, a price still has to be set. Each would-be buyer has a highest price they will pay, and each would-be seller a lowest price they will accept. Rank all buyers' bids from highest to lowest and all sellers' asks from lowest to highest; where the two lines cross is the price at which the most trades take place. Above that price, too few people are willing to buy; below it, too few are willing to sell. Trading at that price also maximizes the combined gains that buyers and sellers get from trade.

Smith (1962) had students play buyers and sellers in the laboratory, each knowing only their own price. Trading prices and volumes soon came close to the levels the theory predicts. Participants did not need to know anyone else's price; bringing the quotes together was enough to find it.

Today's counterpart. Stock exchanges use call auctions at the open and the close: quotes over a period are pooled and matched in one go at the price that maximizes the volume traded.

4. Selling at any time: the market maker and the spread

The market square opens only on the first of the month, and those in a hurry cannot wait. A well-off household in the village steps forward: at any time, it will buy a ship share whose prospects are worth 10 portions for 9.6 portions of grain, and sell one for 10.4. Anyone who wants to sell can sell to it at any time, and anyone who wants to buy can buy from it at any time. It earns the 0.8-portion difference between buying and selling, and bears the risk that the price falls while it holds shares in stock.

A seller in a hurry is in fact choosing between two courses. Willing to wait, they can wait for the first of the month and may meet a buyer who offers more. Unwilling to wait, they sell to this household for 9.6 portions and give up the difference to the party standing ready to trade. The spread is therefore the price paid for trading immediately. How much the household charges depends on how long it expects to hold shares and how much the price may move while it does. The more often shares change hands, the shorter it holds them; the steadier the price, the less risk it bears, and the narrower the spread. If other households are also willing to buy and sell at any time and compete with one another, the spread is pushed down to just what covers these costs.

The spread is the price of immediacy

DisciplineFinance › Market microstructureTypeTheory

Under competition, the bid-ask spread measures the cost of trading immediately without waiting; the party standing ready to trade is compensated by the spread for the time and risk of holding inventory.

Standing in the field
The paper is titled "The Cost of Transacting"; the later market microstructure literature divides traders into suppliers and demanders of immediacy and attributes this distinction to it
Constraint it answers
A seller in a hurry cannot wait for buyers to arrive
Premises
Someone is willing to quote both a buying and a selling price at any time; those who make markets compete with one another; holding inventory carries a time cost and price risk
Proposed by
Demsetz, 1968; the paper notes that a market maker can either act for others or trade on its own account, and in the latter role is compensated by the spread for the risk of holding inventory

The market maker does not know whether those who come to trade already know something; does the spread contain anything else

Today's counterpart. Securities are quoted at a bid and an ask, and the difference between them is the spread; the spread is narrow for actively traded large-company stocks and wider for thinly traded small companies.

5. Liquidity has a price

Since selling costs either time or the spread, buyers deciding what to buy will take this cost into account. Two ships have the same prospects; shares in one can be sold at any time for a small spread, while shares in the other take a long wait and a large discount to sell. Buyers will pay only a lower price for the second, which is to say they demand a higher return.

How much higher depends on who is buying. A household that has to sell once a year, giving up a discount each time, finds hard-to-sell shares very costly; another household plans to hold for twenty years and sells only once in that time, so the same discount spread over each year is small. Hard-to-sell shares therefore flow to those who plan to hold for the long term, and easy-to-sell shares stay with those who often need to buy and sell. Precisely because hard-to-sell shares are held by those who rarely trade, when trading costs rise further, their return need not rise by as much.

Liquidity premium

DisciplineFinance › Asset pricing (liquidity)TypeTheoretical model

An asset's expected return includes compensation for trading costs, and the higher the trading costs, the higher the expected return; assets with high trading costs are held by investors with long holding periods, so the rise in return as trading costs increase gradually becomes smaller.

Standing in the field
The paper is titled "Asset Pricing and the Bid-Ask Spread"; it brought the bid-ask spread into asset pricing, derived expected return as an increasing, concave function of the spread, and proposed the "clientele effect", in which investors with different holding periods each hold assets with different spreads
Constraint it answers
Selling costs time or the spread
Premises
Investors' holding periods differ; trading costs are incurred when selling; investors choose assets by their return after trading costs
Proposed by
Amihud and Mendelson, 1986
Open question
Does how hard an asset is to sell change with market conditions; what happens when everyone is in a hurry to sell

Today's counterpart. Illiquid assets, such as private investments locked up for many years, usually have to offer higher returns; investors who plan to hold for the long term can treat liquidity as something they can sell, in exchange for this compensation.

6. Brokers: finding counterparties for others

There is another kind of person who neither holds shares in stock nor sets prices, but only finds counterparties for others. He knows who wants to sell and who wants to buy, brings the two sides together, and takes a small commission once the trade is done. A broker differs from a market maker: the market maker stands on the other side of the trade itself, while the broker only searches on others' behalf. A broker's value comes from the information he holds and the time he spends, which is exactly what a household lacks when it goes from door to door on its own.

Searching, comparing and bargaining are, at bottom, labour that takes time and thought; the cheaper this labour becomes, the more the form of brokerage will change.

Today's counterpart. Securities brokers accept orders from clients, send them to trading venues for execution and charge a commission; they also choose among venues to get clients a better execution price.

7. The market at the edge of the village: Civilization Simulator, version 8

The simulator unlocks the "Market" rule. Forty households in the village hold ship shares; each year some urgently need grain and must sell, and some have grain to spare and want to buy. You can choose to have each household search on its own, hold a regular market day, or hold a market day plus a market maker. Then watch how many of those in a hurry to sell manage to sell in time, and how large a discount they give up on average.

8. What this step leaves open

With markets, shares can be sold at any time and prices are set by many people together, but new problems follow:

  1. Prices contain information. Someone learns first that the ship has docked safely and buys ahead of everyone else; does the price reflect everything everyone knows?
  2. Those who know more take advantage. The market maker does not know whether a buyer has already heard good news; trading with the informed is a sure loss, so the spread has to cover that loss too.
  3. Ordinary people do not have time to watch the market. Deciding when to buy, and which ship to buy, takes time and expertise.
  4. Who guarantees the rules of the market? Someone has to be responsible for quotes being genuine and for trades being settled as agreed.

9. Implications for products

Securities trading, brokerage and market making all rest on the mechanisms of this chapter. Three implications follow. First, liquidity has a price, paid in waiting time, the spread and discounts; whether a product is easy to sell matters as much as its expected return, and both should be made clear in advance. Second, trading gathers where it is concentrated, and the quality of execution depends on how many buyers and sellers are there; where an order is sent for execution is itself a service that requires professional judgement. Third, searching, comparing and bargaining are labour; once the cost of this labour falls, the division of work between brokers and market makers will change.
