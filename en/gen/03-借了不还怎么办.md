> What is lent is today's grain; what comes back is only a promise about the future. If the borrower does not repay after the harvest and the lender has no recourse, no one will lend in the first place, and all the gains from lending are lost. There are three ways to make a promise credible: make the borrower care about the future, which is reputation; give the borrower something to lose, which is collateral; or let a third party enforce it, which is contracts and rulings. All three have costs, and all three shut some people out. Raising the interest rate cannot replace trust: the higher the rate, the more likely it is that those who come to borrow will not repay.

## 1. Loans not repaid: unable or unwilling

The island has grown more populous, and it has become customary for those who harvest in autumn to lend grain to those who harvest in spring. Yet when spring comes, some people always fail to repay. There are two kinds of failure. One is inability: the spring harvest was poor and the borrower has no grain to give. The other is unwillingness: the grain is in the store, but the borrower does not want to hand it over.

The two kinds of default differ in nature. Inability comes from uncertain harvests and is a matter of risk. This chapter assumes that harvests always arrive as expected and considers only unwillingness. Default then becomes a choice: the borrower weighs what repaying and not repaying would each bring, and decides.

**Today's counterpart.** When lenders assess a borrower, they usually look at two things separately: ability to repay and willingness to repay. The first is judged from income and debts, the second from whether the borrower has kept past agreements.

## 2. In a one-off encounter, not repaying is rational

Imagine two people who will never meet again. When spring comes, the borrower has two choices: repay and lose the principal and interest, or not repay and lose nothing. As long as not repaying has no consequences, it is the better choice. The lender can foresee this, so in autumn he does not lend. Both of them lose the gains from lending, gains that would have been enough to make both better off.

This reasoning reveals the structure of the commitment problem. The question is not whether the borrower is a good person; it is that at the moment of repayment, repaying no longer does him any good. For lending to happen, his gains and losses at that moment must be changed.

**Today's counterpart.** Strangers rarely lend money to each other, and when they do, only small sums.

## 3. Reputation: making the borrower care about the future

If the two must deal with each other year after year, the situation changes. Fail to repay this time, and there will be no loan next time. At the moment of repayment, the borrower is no longer weighing only the principal and interest; he is weighing them against the gains from all future borrowing. As long as those future gains are large enough and the borrower cares enough about the future, repaying is worth more than not repaying.

The force of reputation also depends on how far the record travels. If only the person who was cheated knows about a default, the defaulter can simply borrow from someone else. If the whole village knows, the defaulter will never borrow again.

:::principle Reputation mechanism
When dealings repeat and a default is seen and passed on, the gain from defaulting now cannot outweigh the cooperation lost in the future, so a promise can be credible without outside enforcement.
Discipline: Economics › Game theory (repeated games)
Type: Theoretical model
Standing: Part of the theory of repeated games. In its explanation of the 2005 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences, the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences noted that repeated-game theory had deepened understanding of the prerequisites for cooperation; Aumann shared that prize with Schelling "for having enhanced our understanding of conflict and cooperation through game-theory analysis"
Constraint: Promises may not be kept, and no one can enforce them
Premises: Dealings repeat; records of default can spread; the borrower cares enough about the future
Proposed: Kreps, Milgrom, Roberts and Wilson, 1982. They showed that even when dealings are finite in number, as long as each side is slightly uncertain about the other's type, concern for reputation is enough to sustain cooperation
Question: When records do not travel far and dealings happen only once, reputation loses its force
:::

Traces of this mechanism can be found in history. Greif (1993) used the letters of eleventh-century Maghribi traders in the Mediterranean to argue that traders shared information about who had cheated whom, which kept distant agents from cheating. This interpretation is disputed: Edwards and Ogilvie (2012) argue that these traders also relied heavily on courts and formal contracts.

**Today's counterpart.** Credit records make a single default visible to every lender, and a credit score compresses a history of kept agreements into one number. The easier records are to obtain and the more reliable they are, the stronger the discipline of reputation.

## 4. Collateral: giving the borrower something to lose

Reputation depends on the future; collateral replaces the promise with something present. The borrower pledges a plot of land, an ox or part of his stored grain to the lender, and if he does not repay, the pledged item goes to the lender. As long as the collateral is worth at least the principal and interest owed, not repaying does not pay, and even if the borrower defaults, the lender recovers the loss. Collateral has a second role. The lender can offer two sets of terms at once: more collateral and lower interest, or less collateral and higher interest. Those confident they will repay are not afraid of losing the collateral and choose the first; those who are not confident choose the second. Which one a borrower picks reveals how confident he is.

:::principle Collateral as incentive and screen
Collateral makes a defaulter lose more than default gains; and letting borrowers choose for themselves how much collateral to post against how high an interest rate to pay separates borrowers of different confidence.
Discipline: Economics › Information economics (credit markets)
Type: Theoretical model
Standing: A direct response to the theory of credit rationing, in a paper titled "Screening vs. rationing": as long as collateral and interest rates can be combined, a lender can screen borrowers instead of rationing them
Constraint: Promises may not be kept, and the lender cannot see how confident the borrower is
Premises: The collateral is worth enough and can be disposed of in a default
Proposed: Bester, 1985, who set out the screening role of collateral
Question: Those with nothing to pledge are shut out, and those who most need to borrow are often precisely those with no land and no ox
:::

**Today's counterpart.** Mortgages are secured on the house, car loans on the car, and securities-backed lending and margin trading on the securities held. Young people without assets get worse terms on both the amount they can borrow and the interest rate.

## 5. Third-party enforcement, and the three approaches compared

The third approach is to ask a third party that both sides recognize to enforce the agreement: write it down as a contract, and in case of default let elders, a court or the authorities rule on it and compel the borrower to repay. Third-party enforcement does not depend on the borrower caring about the future, nor does it require him to have something to pledge. But it has its own costs: someone must record contracts, hear disputes and enforce rulings, and the enforcer must itself be trustworthy.

Each of the three approaches has its own range: reputation suits acquaintances who deal repeatedly, collateral suits people with assets, and third-party enforcement suits large transactions between strangers. They can also be used together.

**Today's counterpart.** Loan contracts, notarization and court judgments are third-party enforcement. Lenders usually check credit records, require collateral or a guarantee, and sign a contract, all at once.

## 6. Interest rates cannot replace trust

Since some people do not repay, can the lender simply raise the interest rate and use the extra interest to cover losses from default? Work through how the pool of borrowers changes. As the rate rises, prudent borrowers find the loan no longer worthwhile and drop out first. Those still willing to pay high interest are mostly people planning to take big risks: if the venture succeeds they earn a great deal, and if it fails they do not repay. The higher the rate, the larger the share of such people among those who remain. Beyond a certain point, a further rise brings in less extra interest than it adds in defaults, and the lender's expected return actually falls. The lender therefore prefers not to raise the price, and instead limits whom he lends to and how much.

:::principle Credit rationing
When borrowers' risk cannot be seen, raising the interest rate drives out prudent borrowers and leaves riskier ones; lenders therefore prefer to lend limited amounts at a lower rate rather than raise the price until the supply of and demand for loans are equal.
Discipline: Economics › Information economics (credit markets)
Type: Theoretical model
Standing: The 2001 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences was awarded to Akerlof, Spence and Stiglitz for their analyses of markets with asymmetric information; the prize announcement lists credit rationing among the phenomena explained by this work of Stiglitz
Constraint: Promises may not be kept, and lenders cannot tell borrowers' risk apart
Premises: Lenders cannot distinguish borrowers; borrowers can choose how much risk to take
Proposed: Stiglitz and Weiss, 1981
Question: Whether collateral and reputation can replace screening; how those rationed out can borrow
:::

**Today's counterpart.** Faced with higher-risk applicants, lenders commonly decline the application or lower the credit limit rather than simply raising the interest rate.

## 7. A village that keeps its word: Civilization Simulator, version 3

The simulator unlocks the rule of "Commitment". In the village, twenty households that harvest in spring borrow grain from households that harvest in autumn. Some of them keep their word; the rest are opportunists, who will not repay as long as not repaying has no consequences. You can choose the approach used to make promises credible, and adjust the share of borrowers who keep their word, how much people care about the future and the share who have collateral, then see whether lending can be sustained over twenty years.

With no approach in place, as long as fewer than 80% keep their word, the defaults of the first year frighten lenders away, lending stops at once and all its gains are lost. With reputation, when borrowers care enough about the future, even opportunists repay on time; when they do not care enough, defaulters are excluded in the first year and those who keep their word go on borrowing. With collateral, the 7 households with enough collateral can all borrow, while the other 13 can never borrow, whether or not they would keep their word.

## 8. What this step leaves open

Once promises have become credible, several problems remain unsolved:

1. **Finding the right counterparty is still hard.** The more people there are, the harder it is to match those with surplus grain to those who lack it, and exchange itself has a cost.
2. **Old age is still unsolved.** However credible promises are, the youngest generation cannot find anyone younger to lend to.
3. **Default from inability remains.** This chapter assumed harvests always arrive as expected; once a harvest fails, even the most trustworthy cannot repay.
4. **People without assets are shut out.** Collateral makes lending safe, and also leaves those who most need to borrow unable to.

## 9. Implications for products

Almost every credit product today is some combination of the three approaches in this chapter. Three implications follow. First, a lending product must begin by answering why anyone should believe the borrower will repay; the only answers are reputation, collateral, third-party enforcement, or a combination of them. Second, the force of reputation depends on the reach and reliability of records: the more complete and the harder to forge the records, the more credible the promise and the cheaper the loan. Third, interest rates cannot replace trust. Covering risk by raising the price drives prudent users away; the better course is to improve identification and constraint.
