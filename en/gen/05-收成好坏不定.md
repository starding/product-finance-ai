> Harvests do not always arrive as expected; some years are rich and some poor. Pests may strike only one household's fields, while a drought makes the whole village's harvest fail at once. A household can store extra for bad years on its own, but the larger the reserve, the more it spoils and the more it ties up. When many households share the burden, each household's own bad luck can cancel out, and the more households there are, the smaller the swings each one bears. A disaster that strikes the whole village at once cannot be diversified away; it can only be borne by those willing to bear it, who must be compensated for doing so. Insurance is a state-contingent claim: give up a little in good years and take back a great deal in bad ones. It also brings two new problems: people stop taking care once insured, and those at higher risk are keener to buy cover.

## 1. Harvests rich and poor

For every household on the island, the harvest varies from year to year. In some years the weather is kind and the harvest is larger than usual; in others pests strike and little is gathered. By the logic of diminishing marginal utility, for the same average harvest, the larger the swings, the harder life is: the extra portions of good years cannot make up for the portions lost in bad years.

**Today's counterpart.** Fluctuating income, unexpected medical bills and property losses are risks that every household faces today.

## 2. Bearing it alone: precautionary saving

The most direct remedy is to store more in good years against bad ones. The pain of eating one portion less in a bad year is far greater than the satisfaction of eating one portion more in a good year; and the less one has, the faster the pain of one more portion lost grows. So the harder next year's harvest is to predict, the more valuable the portion kept for a bad year, and the more worthwhile it is to store an extra portion today. The extra saving kept against the unforeseen is called precautionary saving. Leland (1968) showed in a model that, as long as satisfaction has this property (satisfaction measured logarithmically does), the more uncertain future income is, the more people save today. But bearing it alone is costly. To withstand one large crop failure, a household must keep a large reserve year after year; it spoils in the store and cannot be kept as seed or lent to others. Store too little and the household still goes hungry in a bad year; store too much and most of it sits idle in most years.

**Today's counterpart.** A common piece of financial advice is to set aside several months of living expenses as an emergency reserve, which is precautionary saving.

## 3. Bearing it together: mutual insurance

Suppose many households in the village agree that each will put a little grain into a common store every year, and whichever household is struck by pests will be compensated from it. Pest damage in different households is unrelated: this year it is one household, next year another, and what the common store takes in roughly equals what it pays out. The more households there are, the more fully this cancelling works: as long as each household's bad luck is unrelated to the others', the swings per household shrink roughly in proportion to the square root of the number of households.

This kind of mutual aid has a long history. Justinian's *Digest* of Roman law includes the so-called Rhodian sea law: when a ship in a storm jettisons part of its cargo to lighten its load, the loss is shared in proportion by all the owners of goods on board. Townsend (1994), studying household data from three villages in India, found that each household's consumption moved mainly with the village's average consumption and was little affected by its own income, illness or unemployment, which indicates a considerable degree of mutual insurance among villagers. That insurance was not complete, however: in one of the villages, the landless were less well protected.

**Today's counterpart.** An insurer pools the premiums of many policyholders and pays the few among them who suffer a loss. A portfolio holding many stocks lets each company's own bad luck cancel out on the same principle.

## 4. Risk that cannot be diversified away

Mutual insurance has a limit. If a drought makes the whole village's harvest fail at once, the common store takes in little and must pay out much, and no number of households will help. Of each household's risk, the part that belongs to that household alone can be diversified away; the part shared by the whole village cannot be diversified away, however many households join together. From this one can work out what the best sharing arrangement looks like. As long as any household still bears swings that belong to it alone, handing those swings to the common store to be spread among others makes that household's life steadier while barely affecting anyone else, so every household can be better off. Under the best arrangement, therefore, each household's own bad luck is entirely shared away, and what each household finally receives rises and falls only with the village's total harvest.

:::principle Mutuality of risk sharing
Under the most efficient risk-sharing arrangement, what each person finally receives depends only on the total harvest of all; risk belonging to individuals alone is entirely shared away, and each person bears only the aggregate risk.
Discipline: Economics › Economics of uncertainty (risk sharing)
Type: Theorem
Standing: The paper, titled "Equilibrium in a reinsurance market", extended the equilibrium theory of commodity markets to conditions of uncertainty; its characterization of optimal risk exchange is generally known in later literature as Borch's theorem, also called the mutuality principle of risk sharing
Constraint: Harvests are uncertain, and one household cannot bear all the swings
Premises: All parties are risk-averse; all parties agree on how likely each state is; the agreed sharing can be honoured after the fact
Proposed: Borch, 1962, studying the exchange of risk between reinsurers
Question: Who bears the aggregate risk, and how much compensation they must receive; whether such an agreement can last when parties can leave at any time
:::

Risk that cannot be diversified away does not disappear; it can only be borne by someone. Those with deeper reserves, better able to absorb losses, can accept a little grain from others and in a disaster year bear part of others' losses. Those who bear risk must be compensated for it, or no one will be willing to bear it.

**Today's counterpart.** A diversified portfolio still falls when the market as a whole falls. Insurers pass the catastrophe risk they cannot bear on to reinsurers.

## 5. State-contingent claims

Insurance turns risk into a price. Each year the policyholder pays a fixed premium in exchange for a promise: if disaster strikes, he is compensated; if not, he receives nothing. This is a state-contingent claim. Take the idea to its end: drought years, flood years, pest years, bumper years. If for every state there were a claim, paying out only in that state, that could be bought and sold, the households most afraid of drought would buy more of the claim that pays in drought years, while households on high ground, unafraid of drought, would sell that claim in exchange for grain in other states. With each household buying and selling according to its own situation, risk would flow to those most willing and best able to bear it, without a separate common store for every kind of disaster.

:::principle State-contingent claims
If for every possible state there is a claim, paying out only in that state, that can be bought and sold, people can trade to allocate risk to those most willing to bear it and achieve the most efficient sharing of risk.
Discipline: Economics › General equilibrium theory (resource allocation under uncertainty)
Type: Theoretical model
Standing: It extended general equilibrium theory to conditions of uncertainty, and such claims paying out in a single state are generally known as Arrow securities. The 1972 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences was awarded to Hicks and Arrow "for their pioneering contributions to general economic equilibrium theory and welfare theory"
Constraint: Harvests are uncertain, and different people fear different states
Premises: The possible states can be listed in advance; which state occurred can be verified afterwards; for every state there is a corresponding claim that can be bought and sold
Proposed: Arrow, 1953; the paper was published in French in the proceedings of a colloquium on risk theory in Paris, and an English translation was published in 1964
Question: There are too many possible states for each to have its own claim; some states are hard to verify afterwards, or one party knows more about them than the other
:::

A premium has two parts: the expected payout, plus compensation for bearing risk and the costs of running the business. For risk that can be diversified, the compensation can be very low; for risk that cannot, the compensation must be higher.

**Today's counterpart.** Premiums for property, health and life insurance can all be split into the expected payout and a loading for costs. An option is also a state-contingent claim.

## 6. New problems that insurance brings

Insurance solves the sharing of risk, but it brings two new problems. First, once insured, people may no longer be as careful: if the common store compensates for pest damage, why go to the trouble of controlling pests? This is called moral hazard, and Arrow (1963) and Pauly (1968) discussed how it shows up in health insurance. Second, those most eager to insure are often those at highest risk. Households on low-lying land, most afraid of floods, are the keenest to join, and each household knows its own land best, while the keeper of the common store cannot see it all. If everyone pays the same premium, low-risk households find it not worthwhile and leave; those who remain are riskier and riskier, and the premium has to keep rising.

The keeper of the common store cannot see each household's risk, but can let the households reveal it themselves by offering two agreements. One has a low premium, but in a disaster the household first bears part of the loss itself; the other has a high premium and compensates the loss in full. Households on high ground, rarely flooded, do not mind bearing that part themselves and choose the low premium; households on low-lying land, which need compensation almost every year, would rather pay more and choose full compensation. The households' choices sort them into different risk classes. The cost is that, to keep high-risk households from posing as low-risk ones, low-risk households can receive only partial cover.

:::principle Adverse selection in insurance markets
When policyholders know their own risk better than the insurer does, a uniform premium drives out low-risk people; the insurer can offer several contracts with different shares of self-borne loss and let policyholders choose according to their own risk, at the cost that low-risk people receive only partial cover.
Discipline: Economics › Information economics (insurance markets)
Type: Theoretical model
Standing: The 2001 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences was awarded to Akerlof, Spence and Stiglitz "for their analyses of markets with asymmetric information"; the prize materials use this research to show how insurers, through a menu of contracts, let policyholders sort themselves into different risk classes by their own choices
Constraint: Harvests are uncertain, and the insurer cannot see policyholders' risk
Premises: Policyholders know their own risk better than the insurer; insurers compete with one another; contracts can specify both the premium and the amount of cover
Proposed: Rothschild and Stiglitz, 1976; they also showed that such a competitive market may have no equilibrium at all
Question: Low-risk people do not get adequate cover; whether requiring everyone to join would improve matters
:::

The remedies follow from this. Policyholders bear part of the loss themselves, through deductibles and co-insurance, so that they still have an incentive to take precautions and people of different risk sort themselves; premiums are set according to risk, or everyone is required to join, so that only high-risk people do not end up insured.

**Today's counterpart.** Deductibles and co-payment shares in insurance contracts, premiums that vary with age and health, and compulsory social insurance are all responses to these two problems.

## 7. A village of mutual aid: Civilization Simulator, version 5

The simulator unlocks the rule of "Insurance". The village has twenty households, and each harvest varies at random from year to year: part of the variation is each household's own luck, and part is the conditions shared by the whole village. You can choose whether each household keeps its own reserve, joins the mutual granary, or does both, and adjust the weight of village-wide conditions, then see over thirty years how many household-years of hunger there are and how much each household's food varies.

Individual reserves cannot cope with two disaster years in a row, and the mutual granary cannot cope with years when the whole village's harvest fails: relying on the granary alone without reserves, everyone goes hungry at once when the whole village's harvest fails. Diversification and reserves deal with two different kinds of risk, and using both together produces the least hunger.

## 8. What this step leaves open

With insurance, each household's own bad luck can be shared, but several problems remain unsolved:

1. **Savers want access at any time; borrowers need funds for the long term.** The common store and stored grain must both be available at any time, but those clearing land or planting orchards need to borrow for many years.
2. **The risk of a large project is too much for one household.** Building a ship or opening new land can succeed or fail on a large scale; one household can neither put up the resources nor bear the failure.
3. **Who bears the risk that cannot be diversified away, and how much they receive for it.** This requires a way for those willing to bear risk to share in the gains in proportion to their stake.
4. **People take less care once insured, and those at higher risk are keener to insure.** These problems are rooted in asymmetric information and become sharper when managing other people's property on trust.

## 9. Implications for products

Insurance products and diversified investment portfolios are both built on the mechanisms of this chapter. Three implications follow. First, diversification is an almost free improvement: risk that belongs only to individual holdings can be eliminated by diversifying, so there is no need to bear it, and no compensation should be demanded for it. Second, risk that cannot be diversified away does not disappear because of any product; it can only be transferred to those better able to bear it, and at a price. If a product claims to have eliminated such risk, one must ask where the risk has gone. Third, insurance and diversification both face asymmetric information. Their design should leave users with an appropriate share of responsibility and avoid attracting only those at highest risk.
