> More and more of the buying and selling in the market square is done by scribes. They find counterparties for their principals, split large orders, and quote buying and selling prices at any moment. Finding a counterparty costs almost nothing any more, so spreads narrow and ordinary households trade more cheaply. But the scribes come from a few operators, read the same reports and use the same methods, so they reach the same judgment at the same moment. A single fall triggers many scribes to sell at once, and the selling deepens the fall; the scribes that quote prices for others withdraw to protect themselves, precisely when they are most needed. Execution becomes cheaper, yet the market may become more fragile, and so it needs new designs.

## 1. Scribes place orders directly

Finding a counterparty willing to trade used to take time and require concessions on price. Now only one thing changes: more and more of those placing orders for principals and quoting prices in the market are scribes. They can watch many trades at once, quote at any moment, and split a large order into many small ones to sell gradually.

Work through how the spread changes. The spread is the compensation that the party standing ready to trade charges for the time and risk of holding inventory. A scribe quotes at almost no cost and turns its inventory over quickly, so it holds inventory only briefly; many scribes are willing to quote, and they compete with one another, so the spread is pushed very narrow. On calm days, the cost for an ordinary household of buying or selling one ship share falls markedly.

:::rerun The spread is the price of immediacy
Under competition, the bid-ask spread measures the cost of trading immediately without waiting; the party standing ready to trade is compensated through the spread for the time and risk of holding inventory.
Discipline: Finance › Market microstructure
Type: Theory
Standing: The paper is titled "The cost of transacting"; later market microstructure literature divides traders into suppliers and demanders of immediacy and attributes this distinction to the paper
Constraint: Those in a hurry to sell cannot wait for a buyer to arrive
Premises: Someone is willing to quote buying and selling prices at any moment; market makers compete with one another; holding inventory carries a time cost and price risk
Proposed: Demsetz, 1968
Question: Will scribes that quote at any moment keep quoting when risk suddenly rises
Rerun: The cost of quoting and holding inventory drops sharply, market-making scribes multiply, and ordinary spreads narrow greatly; but the premise that "someone is willing to quote at any moment" holds in calm times and may not hold when risk suddenly rises
:::

**Today's counterpart.** The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission's 2010 concept release on market structure stated that estimates of high-frequency trading's share of stock market volume varied widely but were typically 50% or more.

## 2. Scribes that resemble each other

Many households' scribes come from a few operators. They read the same reports, score with the same methods, and even take each other's trades into account. In the past, each household in the market square had its own judgment: some expected prices to rise, others expected them to fall, and their trades offset one another. Now the same news leads many scribes to the same conclusion at the same time.

Even when each scribe has a little information of its own, it may abandon it. Work it through: the first scribe buys on its own information. The second scribe's own information also points up, so it buys. The third scribe's own information points down, but seeing that the first two have both bought, it judges that others know more than it does, and buys as well. From this one on, every later scribe looks only at what those before it did, and its own information no longer enters the price. A long run of buying may rest on nothing more than the little information held by the first two, and a single new piece of news can reverse the whole run.

:::principle Information cascades
When each person can see how those before them acted, there comes a point at which following predecessors is more reasonable than relying on one's own information, and later arrivals stop taking their own information into account; mass conformity may therefore rest on very little information and is easily overturned as a whole by a little new news.
Discipline: Economics › Information economics (social learning)
Type: Theoretical model
Standing: The paper uses "informational cascades" to explain the formation and fragility of fads, fashion and custom; together with Banerjee's "A simple model of herd behavior", published the same year, it is listed by surveys of the field as early work on informational herding
Constraint: Each person holds only a little information and can see what others do
Premises: Actions can be observed but information itself cannot; people decide in sequence; each person's information is limited
Proposed: Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer and Welch, 1992; Banerjee, 1992
Question: Once a cascade has formed, how can the buried information get back into prices
Rerun: Scribes from the same few operators do not merely observe one another's actions; they share the same information and methods; cascades form more easily, and households' judgments are no longer independent of one another
:::

**Today's counterpart.** A 2024 report by the Financial Stability Board noted that artificial intelligence may amplify correlations in markets: many institutions using similar models and data may react in the same way to the same shock.

## 3. Crowding through the same door

Scribes that resemble each other also hold similar ship shares. In normal times this is no problem. But once one of them has to sell for some other reason, the price of the ship shares falls, the other scribes holding the same shares also lose money and cut their positions according to their own rules, and the price falls further. The door is only so wide, and everyone wants to get out at the same time.

Khandani and Lo (2011) studied the collective losses of quantitative hedge funds in August 2007. Many funds used similar long-short equity strategies. The authors infer that the rapid unwinding of one or a few large portfolios pushed down the prices of similar positions, so that other funds holding similar positions suffered large losses at the same time.

**Today's counterpart.** The more similar the strategies and the more concentrated the holdings, the wider the damage spreads when one participant is forced to sell.

{{fig:g17-crowd}}

## 4. Falls that cause falls

Many scribes follow risk rules: cut the position when losses exceed a certain percentage; sell when margin runs short. Each rule is reasonable on its own, but together they form a loop. Prices fall, scribes lose money, and the rules require selling; selling pushes prices down, more scribes lose money, and more selling follows. Scribes that borrowed to buy ship shares are hit harder still: when prices fall, the lender demands more margin, and the scribe has to sell some more.

:::principle Liquidity spirals
When those who make markets and trade depend on borrowed funds, losses from falling prices and tightening margins force them to sell, and selling makes the market harder to trade in and prices lower; funding liquidity and market liquidity reinforce each other, and liquidity can suddenly dry up.
Discipline: Finance › Asset pricing and market microstructure (liquidity)
Type: Theoretical model
Standing: The paper, titled "Market liquidity and funding liquidity", distinguishes a "margin spiral" caused by tightening margins from a "loss spiral" caused by losses on positions, and shows that when capital is ample the market is in a liquid equilibrium, but once capital falls far enough it can suddenly shift into a low-liquidity equilibrium
Constraint: Selling requires finding a counterparty, and the parties willing to take the other side are constrained by funding
Premises: Traders depend on borrowed funds; margins vary with market volatility; traders hold similar positions
Proposed: Brunnermeier and Pedersen, 2009
Question: How can a spiral be broken before it forms
Rerun: Scribes enforce risk rules to the letter and resemble one another, so more selling is triggered at the same moment and the spiral turns faster
:::

**Today's counterpart.** When prices fall sharply, margin calls and forced liquidations tend to cluster, which deepens the fall.

## 5. Liquidity vanishes when it is most needed

The scribes that quote prices in normal times are the first to withdraw when risk suddenly rises. They do not know why prices are falling, the risk of holding inventory has suddenly grown, and the safest course is to stop quoting or to pass on the ship shares they have just bought as quickly as possible. So the spread, narrow in normal times, becomes astonishingly wide just when trading is most needed.

The joint report by the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission and the Commodity Futures Trading Commission on the "Flash Crash" of 6 May 2010 records the following. A mutual fund used an algorithm to sell 75,000 S&P 500 index futures contracts, worth about $4.1 billion. The algorithm sold at 9% of the previous minute's trading volume, taking account only of volume, not of price or time. High-frequency traders first bought, then rapidly passed contracts among themselves, trading more than 27,000 contracts within 14 seconds while buying only about 200 on net. Buy-side order book depth fell to less than 1% of its level that morning, and futures prices fell a further 1.7% in 15 seconds. After the exchange triggered an automatic mechanism that paused trading for 5 seconds, selling pressure eased and prices began to recover. Using that day's trading records, Kirilenko, Kyle, Samadi and Tuzun (2017) found that high-frequency traders did not trigger the crash, but their responses amplified the volatility.

**Today's counterpart.** After the Flash Crash, the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission approved the "limit up–limit down" mechanism in 2012 (in effect from 2013) and revised the market-wide circuit breaker rules.

## 6. Giving the market time to breathe

Since spirals come from selling in the same direction at the same moment, the way to break them is to slow time down. When prices fall too far within a very short time, trading is halted, giving scribes time to reassess and giving those willing to buy at low prices time to arrive. Continuous matching can be replaced by matching in a batch at very short intervals, so that being a moment faster loses its advantage (Budish, Cramton and Shim, 2015). Another approach is to keep participants diverse: the more scribes there are with different methods, the less likely a single piece of news is to set off action in the same direction.

Halts have costs too. Those who want to sell cannot sell for a while, and prices may jump further when the halt ends. The threshold and length of a halt must balance breaking the spiral against obstructing trade.

**Today's counterpart.** The current market-wide circuit breakers in U.S. stock markets trigger when the S&P 500 index falls 7%, 13% and 20% from the previous day's close. Under the single-stock limit up–limit down rule, trading pauses for 5 minutes when the price stays at the limit band for more than 15 seconds.

## 7. A market of scribes: Civilization Simulator, version 17

The simulator unlocks "Agents as market participants". Forty scribes buy and sell the same ship share in the market square; some of them come from the same operator and use the same method. All of them follow a rule of cutting positions when losses mount. One day, a large holder's scribe must sell a big block of ship shares. You can adjust the share of scribes from the same operator and switch the circuit breaker on or off, then watch how low the price falls that day and how quickly it recovers.

## 8. What this step leaves open

Once scribes become the main market participants, several questions remain unresolved:

1. **Human bias.** Can scribes help principals stick to their plans and not sell along with everyone else in a panic? Or will they be used to exploit human biases?
2. **Sources of diversity.** Diversity among participants reduces action in the same direction, but who has an incentive to maintain that diversity?
3. **Coordinating rules.** Each scribe's risk rules are reasonable, yet together they form a spiral. Who looks at the whole picture?
4. **What does not change.** Ships can still sink; the risk of a fall does not disappear because of scribes.

## 9. Implications for products

As more and more of users' trades are executed by agents, a product faces not only the risk to each individual user but also the risk of many agents acting at once. Three implications follow. First, the fall in execution costs is real, but it shows up mainly on calm days; the product should let users know that in violent swings the price they get may be far from the usual one. Second, an agent's risk rules should not look only at a single user's account; they should also avoid firing at the same moment as large numbers of similar agents. Building delays, batching and price protection into the rules is safer than a hair trigger. Third, the more similar agents' methods and data are, the more fragile the market becomes. For a product, preserving each user's own agreement and differences, rather than putting every user on the same strategy, is itself a contribution to the market.
