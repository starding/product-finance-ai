> A year has passed. The villager opens the ledger and sees that the retirement account is up 10%. Is this the result of the scribe keeping to the agreement, or did it take risks outside the agreement and happen to win? Conversely, if the account were down 10%, would that be a bad run of years or a breach by the scribe? From results alone he cannot tell. For him to see the process, the records must be verifiable; otherwise they are only the scribe's own account. Verification takes time, so not every entry can be checked. Records must be layered so that he can understand them though he looks only a few times a year. They must state what was known at the time, so that decisions are not judged afterwards by their outcomes. And every step must state which clause of the agreement it relies on.

## 1. Results alone cannot tell good from bad

A 10% gain in a year may mean the scribe kept the agreement and the years were good; it may also mean it quietly put the house money into ship shares and happened to catch a good season for the fleet. A 10% loss may mean a storm, or it may mean a breach. The swings of good and bad years are far larger than the scribe's influence, so judged by results alone, good and bad scribes are almost indistinguishable over a year or two. A principal who cannot tell them apart can only extend trust at the average level: good scribes receive less trust than they deserve, and bad ones are overrated.

There is one new constraint: the user cannot see the agent's process, and information is asymmetric.

**Today's counterpart.** A fund's one-year performance depends largely on that year's market; the same return may come from very different levels of risk.

## 2. Verifiable records

Suppose each scribe can choose whether to make its records public, and public records are kept by a party unconnected with it and cannot be altered. Work through what happens. The scribes that keep the agreement most strictly will certainly disclose, because disclosure sets them apart. Among those that remain undisclosed, the better ones will also disclose; otherwise principals will lump them together with worse ones. Round after round, as long as public records can be verified and disclosure costs little, almost every scribe discloses, and not disclosing becomes bad news in itself.

:::principle Unravelling in information disclosure
When the seller's information can be verified and disclosure costs little, the highest-quality sellers disclose voluntarily to set themselves apart, the others follow one by one, and in the end almost everything is disclosed; buyers therefore read non-disclosure as bad news.
Discipline: Economics › Information economics (voluntary disclosure)
Type: Theoretical model
Standing: Grossman and Milgrom each reached this result in 1981, and the two papers are often cited together as the foundation of voluntary disclosure theory; one of Milgrom's applications is that buyers will assume any product information a salesperson withholds is unfavourable to the product
Constraint: The user cannot see the process, and the agent knows more than the user
Premises: Disclosed information can be verified; disclosure costs little; buyers know the seller holds the information
Proposed: Grossman, 1981; Milgrom, 1981
Question: Will real principals treat silence as bad news
:::

Experiments support this reasoning only in part. In the experiments of Jin, Luca and Martin (2021), receivers of information were often not sceptical enough about non-disclosure, so senders did not always disclose; full disclosure emerged only gradually when receivers were told after each round what had been withheld. For verifiable records to work, the product must also help the user read "no record" as a signal.

**Today's counterpart.** Listed companies must publish audited financial reports at regular intervals; refusing or delaying disclosure in itself puts investors on alert.

## 3. Verification has a cost

That records can be verified does not mean every entry must be. Checking several hundred actions a year one by one would take a great deal of his time, and the labour of another scribe as well. Work through where verification should be spent. When results meet the agreement and every indicator is within its boundaries, a detailed check yields little; when results are poor or a condition in the agreement has been triggered, a detailed check yields the most. Add unannounced random spot checks in ordinary times, and the scribe cannot predict which entry will be examined, so the temptation to breach the agreement shrinks.

:::principle Costly state verification
When it is costly for the principal to observe the outcome the agent reports, the optimal agreement verifies only when the reported outcome is poor; under certain conditions such an agreement takes the form of a standard debt contract: if the agreed repayment is made there is no verification, and only when it cannot be made is there verification and takeover.
Discipline: Economics › Contract theory (financial contracting)
Type: Theoretical model
Standing: The paper derives the optimal contract when verification is costly; later literature uses it, together with Gale and Hellwig (1985), to show that debt contracts induce truthful reporting while minimizing verification costs
Constraint: Verification is costly, and the principal cannot check every entry
Premises: The cost of verification can be estimated in advance; when the agent reports truthfully, the principal need not verify
Proposed: Townsend, 1979
Question: If verification happens only when results are poor, what about breaches hidden in good results
Rerun: The scribe's records are almost free, and another scribe can carry out most of the verification for the principal, so the cost of verification falls sharply; verification can therefore be triggered not only by "poor results" but by "poor results, a triggered condition, or random selection"
:::

**Today's counterpart.** A financial audit does not examine every transaction. It sets priorities by risk and then tests samples; the International Standards on Auditing contain specific provisions on audit sampling.

## 4. Records he can understand: layering

The scribe carries out several hundred actions a year, each one recorded, but he looks only a few times a year for half an hour each time. Putting the full record in front of him is the same as giving him nothing. Work out how to arrange it so he can understand it. The top layer says in one sentence whether each account has moved closer to or further from its target over the period and whether any boundary has been crossed. The second layer lists a few key points: the decisions that were large, irreversible or set off a trigger. Only the bottom layer holds the complete entry-by-entry record, for him or whoever checks on his behalf to examine in depth. He can stop at any layer.

:::rerun A wealth of information creates a poverty of attention
Information consumes the attention of its recipients; the richer the information, the scarcer the attention, so organizations and systems should be designed to economize on the recipient's attention rather than to supply more information.
Discipline: Management and economics › Organization and decision theory
Type: Theory
Standing: Simon made this point in a paper on designing organizations for an information-rich world; its phrase "a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention" is often regarded as the origin of the term "attention economy"; Simon received the 1978 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences "for his pioneering research into the decision-making process within economic organizations"
Constraint: There is more information than can be read, and time for reading it is limited
Premises: Recipients' attention is limited; processing information costs the recipient time
Proposed: Simon, 1971
Question: Who writes the summary for layered records, and could the summary hide bad news in a lower layer
Rerun: In a product, records should be layered by attention: one sentence, key points, the full record; the summary must be traceable to every layer beneath it, or it is merely another one-sided account
:::

Ben-Shahar and Schneider (2014) examined the effects of mandated disclosure systematically and concluded that it usually fails: most people skip the disclosures and lack the ability to analyse them, while legislators keep adding disclosure requirements. The amount disclosed is not in itself the answer.

**Today's counterpart.** In 2019 the US Securities and Exchange Commission required broker-dealers and investment advisers to give retail clients a relationship summary of no more than two pages, or no more than four pages for firms offering both kinds of service.

## 5. What was known at the time

Looking back after a storm, the scribe's purchase of ship shares three days before it seems foolish; yet three days before, nobody knew the storm was coming. Judge decisions by outcomes learned afterwards, and good decisions are blamed for bad luck while bad ones are praised for good luck. To judge fairly, the record cannot say only what was done; it must also say what was known at the time: which news had been seen, which clause of the agreement called for the action, and how large the risk was estimated to be then.

Fischhoff's (1975) experiments found that people who know an outcome overestimate how likely it was beforehand, and are unaware of this influence.

**Today's counterpart.** Trade records usually capture the time of the order, the price and the market conditions at that moment, so that later review can reconstruct the situation at the instant the order was placed.

## 6. Why this was done

An entry reading "Sold 2 ship shares" tells him nothing about whether it was right. Written as "Sold 2 ship shares: the retirement account's ship-share proportion had risen to 86% (agreed 80%, a deviation of more than five percentage points); brought back per clause 3 of the agreement", it lets him see at a glance whether the step kept to the agreement. When every step states which clause it relies on and what the figures were at the time, the record turns from a running log into evidence that can be checked item by item. A step for which no basis can be given is precisely the one he should question.

**Today's counterpart.** The EU's General Data Protection Regulation provides that, for decisions based solely on automated processing, the data subject is entitled to meaningful information about the logic involved, as well as the significance and the envisaged consequences of such processing.

## 7. A visible process: Civilization Simulator, version 22

The simulator unlocks "process records and verifiable evidence". Over ten years the scribe occasionally breaches the agreement: it crosses a boundary, takes risks outside the agreement, or makes trades that conflict with his interests. You can choose how records are kept: no records, a summary written by the scribe itself, or layered records kept by a third party and open to verification. Then choose how they are checked: never, every entry, or detailed checks when results are poor plus random spot checks. See, over the ten years, how many breaches are caught, how much of his time is spent, and whether he mistakes a good scribe for a bad one.

## 8. What this step leaves open

Once the process can be seen and verified, several problems remain:

1. **After a breach is found.** If the scribe is found to have crossed a boundary, who bears the loss, and how is it compensated?
2. **Errors within the boundaries.** If the scribe keeps the agreement fully but misjudges, whose consequences are they?
3. **Irreversible actions.** Some mistakes cannot be undone however clearly they are recorded. How can they be stopped before they happen?
4. **A new scribe.** A scribe that has just opened for business has no record. On what basis can he begin to trust it?

## 9. Implications for products

For a wealth-management product built around an agent, the third part that has to grow is "process records and verifiable evidence". Three implications follow. First, records should be kept by a party unconnected with the agent, be unalterable, and be open to the user by default; verifiable records make agents that keep their agreements willing to be seen, and turn "no record" into a signal. Second, records should be layered by the user's attention: one sentence on how far each goal is and whether any boundary was crossed, then the key points, and only then everything; each layer must be traceable to the one below. Third, every step should state what was known at the time, which clause of the agreement it relies on, and what the figures were then; verification takes place in three situations, poor results, a triggered condition and random selection, with the agent doing most of the checking for the user, who looks only at conclusions and exceptions.
