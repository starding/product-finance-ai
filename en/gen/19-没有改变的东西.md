> The scribe makes research, checking and execution almost free, lets every household afford a personal agent, and can even ask people to wait when they panic. Yet there are a few things it cannot change at all. Grain is still harvested once a year, and saving enough grain for old age still takes decades. Diversification removes only the bad luck of a single household; a drought that strikes the whole village still comes, and only those who bear it are compensated. Some losses, once they occur, can never be undone. Any constraint that survives the rerun is not one this technology can solve, and these are precisely the problems finance was first meant to handle.

## 1. Grain is still harvested once a year

The scribe cannot make the harvest come early, nor change when a lifetime's income arrives. People earn more when young and nothing when old, whoever keeps the accounts. Saving enough grain for old age still means starting young and saving year by year. Compounding needs time; what the scribe can save is fees and mistakes, and it cannot shrink thirty years into three.

Consider what the scribe can do here. It can work out a household's income and spending over a lifetime, set how much to save each year according to the lifetime level, and carry this out on time every year. The reason for smoothing consumption has not changed, nor has the time smoothing takes. Only the premise that "people act on their plans" has changed: it is easier to satisfy than before.

:::rerun Life-cycle and permanent-income hypotheses
People plan their consumption according to their lifetime or long-run income, not their current income; temporary highs and lows in income are absorbed mainly by saving and by drawing on savings.
Discipline: Economics › Macroeconomics (theory of consumption and saving)
Type: Hypothesis
Standing: Modigliani received the 1985 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences "for his pioneering analyses of saving and of financial markets", and the prize announcement lists the life-cycle hypothesis of household saving, developed with Brumberg, as his foremost contribution; Friedman received the 1976 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences, and the first item in the citation is his achievements in the field of consumption analysis
Constraint: Income arrives at a few points in time, while consumption is spread over every day
Premises: Satisfaction grows at a diminishing rate; today's income can be kept for the future, or future income used in advance; people act on their plans
Proposed: Modigliani and Brumberg, 1954; Friedman, 1957
Question: An agent can help people act on their plans, but it cannot change when income and needs arrive
Rerun: The mismatch in time between income and consumption remains exactly as before, and so does the number of years accumulation takes; the scribe only makes the premise that "people act on their plans" easier to satisfy
:::

**Today's counterpart.** The outcome of retirement saving depends largely on how early one starts and how many years one keeps it up.

## 2. Ships can still sink

The scribe can spread each household's ship shares across every ship in the village at almost no cost. Diversification removes the bad luck that belongs to one ship alone: this ship strikes a reef, that one comes home fully laden, and together the result is steady. But however well spread the holdings are, diversification cannot remove a storm that strikes the whole village's ships at once. The more ships one holds, the closer the portfolio's fluctuation comes to that of the village fleet as a whole, but it never falls below it.

:::rerun Mutuality of risk sharing
Under the most efficient risk-sharing arrangement, each person's final share depends only on the total harvest of everyone; risks that belong to individuals are fully shared away, and each person bears only the aggregate risk.
Discipline: Economics › Economics of uncertainty (risk sharing)
Type: Theorem
Standing: The paper, titled "Equilibrium in a reinsurance market", extends the theory of equilibrium in commodity markets to conditions of uncertainty; its characterization of optimal risk exchange is commonly known in later literature as "Borch's theorem", also called the mutuality principle of risk sharing
Constraint: Harvests are uncertain, and one household cannot bear all the ups and downs
Premises: Everyone is risk-averse; everyone agrees on the probabilities of the various states; the agreed sharing can be honoured afterwards
Proposed: Borch, 1962
Question: Who bears the aggregate risk, and how much compensation must the bearer receive?
Rerun: The scribe drives the cost of sharing individual risk towards zero, so every household can come close to the best sharing arrangement; the aggregate risk is not reduced at all and must still be borne by the whole village
:::

Statman (1987) calculated portfolios made up of randomly selected stocks: once trading costs are taken into account, a well-diversified portfolio needs at least thirty to forty stocks, and however many stocks it holds, its risk never falls below that of the market as a whole.

**Today's counterpart.** An index fund that holds every stock in proportion to its market share has already removed almost all individual risk, yet when the market as a whole falls, it falls too.

## 3. Only bearing risk earns compensation

Since individual risk can be diversified away almost for free, the market will not pay anyone for bearing it: whoever fails to diversify bears, for nothing, a risk others need not bear. Only the part that cannot be diversified away, the part that rises and falls with the whole village fleet, needs compensation. The more a ship share moves with the fleet, the higher the return its holders demand.

At this point it becomes clear what the scribe cannot do. It cannot create a ship share with a high return that does not move with the whole village. If one existed, every scribe would buy it, its price would be bid up at once, and its return would fall accordingly. An agent that promises "high returns with no risk" is either hiding risk or has made an error in its calculations.

:::principle Capital asset pricing model
In a market where all investors can diversify fully, the expected return of an asset depends only on how much it moves with the market as a whole, that is, on its systematic risk; individual risk can be diversified away and therefore earns no compensation.
Discipline: Finance › Asset pricing
Type: Theoretical model
Standing: The 1990 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences was awarded to Markowitz, Miller and Sharpe "for their pioneering work in the theory of financial economics"; the prize announcement summarizes Sharpe's contribution as a theory of price formation for financial assets, the capital asset pricing model
Constraint: Future returns are uncertain, and risk cannot be fully diversified away
Premises: Investors are risk-averse and can diversify fully; investors agree on the distribution of returns; they can borrow and lend at the same rate
Proposed: Sharpe, 1964
Question: What determines how much systematic risk a household should bear?
Rerun: The scribe makes full diversification almost free, so the model's premises are closer to reality than before; systematic risk must still be borne by someone, and compensation still goes only to those who bear it
:::

The model's empirical record is not good. Fama and French (2004) note that its empirical performance is poor enough to invalidate many of its applications; the problem may lie in its simplifying assumptions or in the difficulty of testing it. But the reasoning that "only risk that cannot be diversified away needs compensation, and diversifiable risk earns none" does not depend on the specific form of the model.

**Today's counterpart.** The guiding opinions on asset management issued in 2018 by the People's Bank of China and other authorities prohibit financial institutions from promising principal protection or guaranteed returns in their asset management business.

## 4. Some losses cannot be undone

One kind of loss differs from all others: once it occurs, there is no chance to make it good. An old man draws grain from his savings every year to live on. If a sharp fall happens to come in the first few years of his retirement, the ship shares he sells at the bottom cannot wait for prices to recover; with the same run of years in reverse order, his old age is far more comfortable. A household that borrows to buy ship shares and is forced to sell when they fall is no longer in the game when prices later return to where they were.

Consider what this means for decisions. If a decision can be withdrawn at any time, one may as well act first and see. If it cannot be withdrawn once made, and news about the future keeps arriving, then keeping options open has value in itself: do nothing yet, or do a little less, and decide when there is more information. The scribe can make every step faster, but it cannot make an irreversible decision reversible.

:::principle Irreversibility effect
When a decision cannot be withdrawn and information about its consequences arrives over time, keeping future options open has value; ignoring that value leads people to make irreversible decisions too early or too extensively.
Discipline: Economics › Decision-making under uncertainty (environmental and investment economics)
Type: Theoretical model
Standing: Arrow and Fisher, and Henry, each reached this conclusion in 1974, and later literature treats the two papers together as the origin of the "irreversibility effect"; Arrow and Fisher also introduced the concept of "quasi-option value"
Constraint: The future is uncertain, and some losses cannot be undone once they occur
Premises: The decision cannot be withdrawn, or withdrawing it is very costly; information about the consequences arrives over time
Proposed: Arrow and Fisher, 1974; Henry, 1974
Question: Which actions are irreversible, and who confirms them?
Rerun: The scribe makes execution almost instantaneous, so irreversible decisions can be made in less time; the value of keeping options open has not fallen, and must now be deliberately preserved in the design
:::

**Today's counterpart.** For withdrawals in retirement, the same average return can lead to very different outcomes depending on the order of good and bad years. When a margin account is forcibly liquidated in a sharp price fall, the loss is fixed even if prices recover afterwards.

## 6. What this step leaves open

At this point in the rerun, time, risk and irreversible losses have not changed; what has changed is the cost of delegation, checking, information and execution. What remains are the questions a product must answer:

1. **What to promise.** A household has its own goals, time horizon and capacity to bear risk. What can an agent promise it, and what can it not?
2. **The user is not present.** When an agent keeps working on someone's behalf, what must be agreed in advance?
3. **The user cannot see the process.** How can users understand and check what the agent has done?
4. **Agents make mistakes.** How can irreversible consequences be capped, require confirmation and be compensated?
5. **Trust takes time.** On what basis does a user begin to trust a new agent, and how is that trust built step by step?

## 7. Implications for products

The easiest mistake for an agent-centred wealth product is to take the collapse in the cost of cognitive labour for the disappearance of finance's basic problems. Three implications follow. First, what a product can improve is fees, mistakes and execution; what it cannot improve is time, systematic risk and irreversible losses. It should promise users only the former. Second, a product must explain systematic risk clearly: how much the user bears, how much compensation that may bring, and what the worst run of years would look like. Third, every irreversible action must be deliberately slowed down, leave room for options, and be confirmed by the user in person; the faster the execution, the more it needs to stop at these points.
