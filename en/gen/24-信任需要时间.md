> Promises, the agreement, records, defences and compensation are all in place, yet the villager still hesitates. The scribe has only just opened for business; he has never seen it work. Why should he hand it the grain for his house and his retirement? Trust is not given all at once. He needs to believe three things: that it is capable, that it has his interests at heart, and that it does not say one thing and do another. Each has its own evidence. A scribe without a record can borrow signals that others cannot afford: the operator's capital, insurance and licence. Authority starts small and is widened by one level each time the agreement has been kept for a period, and the record of keeping the agreement gradually changes his judgement of the scribe. Trust is lost faster than it is built. After a mistake, trust should step back rather than drop to zero at once, and the amount of trust should always match how reliable the scribe actually is.

## 1. What he needs to believe

"Trusting the scribe" contains three different things. The first is believing that it is capable: it can keep accounts, understands the fleet, and does not make elementary mistakes. The second is believing that it has his interests at heart: its pay comes from him, not from the people who sell ship shares, and what it recommends is what he would choose himself after thinking it through. The third is believing that its words and actions match: it really keeps the boundaries written in the agreement, and the grounds written in the records really exist. Each has its own evidence: capability is judged by the past record, whose interests it serves by how it is paid and what its goals are, and consistency between words and actions by the agreement and its verification. If any one is missing, trust cannot stand.

There is one new constraint: the user has no prior about a new agent, and trust takes time.

:::principle Three sources of trustworthiness
Trust is one party's willingness to be exposed to risk from another party's actions without being able to monitor or control that party. How trustworthy the other party is depends on three things, ability, benevolence and integrity, and how much trust is given also depends on the principal's own propensity to trust.
Discipline: Management › Organizational behaviour (trust)
Type: Theoretical model
Standing: The paper defines trust as "willingness to be vulnerable" and distinguishes trust from the other party's trustworthiness; a later meta-analysis found that ability, benevolence, integrity and trust propensity each affect trust independently
Constraint: The principal cannot monitor the agent at all times, yet must entrust it with important matters
Premises: The object of trust can be judged by observable behaviour and arrangements; the principal has some propensity to trust
Proposed: Mayer, Davis and Schoorman, 1995
Question: For a newcomer without a record, what proves each of the three?
:::

**Today's counterpart.** The relationship summary that the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission requires of brokers and investment advisers must describe the services offered, fees and costs, conflicts of interest, and whether there is a disciplinary history. These correspond roughly to ability, benevolence and integrity.

## 2. Borrowed trust

A scribe that has just opened for business has no record, so there is no way to prove its ability. It can borrow trust from others: the operator that deploys it puts up capital as a deposit, buys insurance against its errors, obtains a licence, and has its accounts audited by a third party. What matters about all of these is that they are expensive. An operator that intends to cheat cannot afford such a cost, or would find it not worth paying. Precisely because they are costly, they can separate the reliable from the unreliable. Saying "we are trustworthy" costs nothing, and so it proves nothing.

:::principle Signalling
A party with private information can credibly convey its type by taking an action that would be too costly for a low-quality type. As long as the cost of the action differs by type, different types will make different choices, and the market can tell them apart.
Discipline: Economics › Information economics
Type: Theoretical model
Standing: Using education as an example, the paper shows that even if education does not raise productivity, it can separate job seekers of high and low ability. For this Spence shared the 2001 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences with Akerlof and Stiglitz "for their analyses of markets with asymmetric information"
Constraint: A new agent has no record, and the principal cannot see what type it is
Premises: The cost of the signal differs by type; the principal can observe the signal
Proposed: Spence, 1973
Question: The signal vouches for the operator; the scribe's own ability still has to be shown by its record
:::

**Today's counterpart.** Securities and investment advisory businesses are licensed, and firms are subject to requirements on capital, personnel and internal controls.

## 3. Starting small

Even with borrowed trust, there is no need to hand everything over at the start. Work it through to see the cheapest way to begin. First give the scribe the smallest task, such as investing on schedule each month, where one mistake can lose at most a small portion. After it has kept the agreement for a while, widen its authority by one level and let it adjust the proportions as the agreement provides. After another period of keeping the agreement, let it handle the rebalancing after a storm. At each level, the loss limit matches the confidence he has already built up. A scribe that intends to cheat must first do small tasks honestly for a long time before it gets a task large enough to be worth cheating on, and during that time its record has long been in front of him.

:::principle Starting small
In a long-term relationship where each side is unsure whether the other is reliable, starting the scale of dealings small and increasing it as cooperation continues is an arrangement that makes both sides willing to keep cooperating; the growth in scale makes early betrayal unprofitable for an unreliable party.
Discipline: Economics › Game theory (relational contracts)
Type: Theoretical model
Standing: The paper studies long-term relationships in which the two parties jointly decide the scale of dealings and each chooses to cooperate or betray; it proves that under a strong renegotiation condition, "starting small" is the unique equilibrium selected
Constraint: The principal has no prior about a new agent, and the agent may be unreliable
Premises: The scale of dealings can be adjusted step by step; each side can observe whether the other betrays
Proposed: Watson, 1999
Question: How much to widen at each level, and how long the agreement must be kept before widening
Rerun: Applied to products, the boundaries of authority are widened level by level as the record of keeping the agreement builds up: at each higher level, more matters can be handled on its own and the limit on what a single action can move is higher, with each level's limit written down in advance; large matters that cannot be undone always require confirmation first
:::

**Today's counterpart.** Many places have graduated licensing for new drivers: they first drive under restrictions, and the restrictions are lifted step by step as conditions are met. A Cochrane Collaboration systematic review concluded that such schemes are associated with fewer crashes among young drivers, and that the effect is more pronounced where restrictions are stronger.

## 4. The record gradually changes the judgement

His judgement of the scribe is not simply "trust" or "distrust" but a probability: how likely it is to be the reliable type. Each time it keeps the agreement, the probability moves up a little; each time it breaches the agreement, it drops a long way. The starting probability depends on the borrowed signals; changes after that depend on the record. With a reliable scribe, given a long enough record of keeping the agreement, his judgement will eventually approach the truth. An unreliable scribe that wants to keep its reputation must keep the agreement for a long time, and that in itself reduces the harm it can do.

:::principle Building a reputation
When it is uncertain whether one party is a "keeps-its-word type", the other party continually revises its judgement on the basis of past behaviour. Even when the number of interactions is finite, the wish to keep a reputation as a keeps-its-word type can lead a party that has an incentive to betray to keep its word for a long time.
Discipline: Economics › Game theory (reputation theory)
Type: Theoretical model
Standing: The paper responds to Selten's "chain-store paradox", showing that a small amount of uncertainty about one party's type is enough for the intuitively expected reputation effect to appear even in a finite number of interactions. Together with the paper by Milgrom and Roberts in the same issue of the same journal, it explains reputation through uncertainty about types
Constraint: The principal cannot see the agent's type, only its behaviour
Premises: The principal has an initial judgement about the agent's type; the agent's behaviour can be observed and recorded
Proposed: Kreps and Wilson, 1982; Milgrom and Roberts, 1982
Question: How long does the judgement take to approach the truth, and how much should one breach lower it?
:::

**Today's counterpart.** Credit scores are updated monthly according to repayment history: on-time repayments raise the score slowly, while one serious delinquency lowers it sharply.

## 5. Lost faster than it is built

One instance of overstepping can wipe out years of keeping the agreement. People react far more strongly to bad news than to good news, and this is not wrong in itself: a single breach often reveals much more information than a single instance of keeping the agreement. But if every mistake sent trust back to zero, a single misjudgement or one small execution error by the scribe would put him back at the starting point. All the authority built up over years would be withdrawn, and he would return to his own, worse judgement.

Work it through to see what a reasonable response to mistakes looks like. First, look at what kind of mistake it is. Overstepping and concealment suggest the scribe may be the unreliable type, and its authority should be largely withdrawn. For a small execution error that has already been compensated, with its cause found and fixed, the authority steps back only one level. A misjudgement is a risk the agreement has accepted, so there is no need to withdraw authority, but the agreement should be reviewed to see whether it needs to change. Only by stepping back rather than dropping to zero can trust keep accumulating after a mistake.

Slovic (1993), discussing risk perception and trust, argued that negative events affect trust far more than positive ones, so that trust is easily lost and hard to build. Experiments by White and Eiser (2005) found that this asymmetry is less pronounced when the information is less specific and the risk is lower.

**Today's counterpart.** China's motor vehicle driving licences use a points system: each cycle allows 12 points, and each violation adds 1 to 12 points depending on its seriousness. A driver who reaches 12 points must attend a full-points study course and pass an examination, while serious violations such as drunk driving lead to revocation of the licence under the law.

## 7. After this step

Five constraints, five parts: what to promise; mandate parameters and authority; process records and verifiable evidence; limits, confirmation and compensation; and the path to trust. Together they make up a financial product that can stand in a world where cognitive labour is almost free.

## 8. Implications for products

For a wealth-management product built around an agent, the last part to grow is "the path to trust". Three implications follow. First, provide separate evidence for ability, benevolence and integrity: ability rests on verifiable records, benevolence on charging the user and writing down the goals, and integrity on the agreement and independent verification. Second, a new agent first borrows expensive signals, namely the operator's capital, insurance, licence and audit, then starts small and has its authority widened level by level as its record of keeping the agreement builds up, with the loss limit at each level written down in advance. Third, adjust trust according to the kind of mistake: overstepping and concealment lead to a large withdrawal of authority, an execution error that has been compensated and fixed steps back only one level, and a misjudgement does not withdraw authority; the amount of trust always matches how reliable the agent actually is.
