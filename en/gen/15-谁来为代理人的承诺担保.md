> A scribe can buy and sell ship shares for a household, adjust its plans, and even draw grain out of the granary. It will make mistakes, and others may exploit it. Yet when it errs, punishing it achieves nothing: it has no property from which to pay compensation and no long-built name to lose, and it can start over under a new name. Of the three devices that grew out of lending, neither collateral nor reputation can attach to it, and a third party's judgment can take nothing from it. The only device left is to hold the people behind it responsible. So the arrangements must change. Whoever deploys a scribe bears liability for compensation and insures against it; every step the scribe takes leaves a record kept by someone else; the scribe receives only the least authority its task requires, and the assets stay with a custodian who has nothing to do with it.

## 1. A scribe has nothing to lose

A promise is credible because the party who breaks it loses something: future cooperation, the property pledged as collateral, or the grain a judgment orders it to pay. A scribe acting for a principal must also make promises: to buy and sell according to the agreed plan, not to exceed its authority, and not to hand the principal's grain to anyone else. But it has no property, and it does not care about the future.

Consider what punishment does. Someone who manages grain for others and must make good any loss in full will check his work carefully. But if all his property amounts to only a small part of the loss, a judgment ordering full compensation can take only what he has. For him, the worst outcome is losing that small amount of property, so he decides how much care to take according to that amount, not according to the loss the principal might suffer. The less property he has, the less he cares. Nor will he insure against other people's losses of his own accord, because he would pay the premium while the principal received the benefit. A scribe is the extreme case of this reasoning: its property is zero.

:::principle The judgment-proof problem
When a potential injurer's assets are smaller than the harm he may cause, liability can take only what he owns; he therefore sets his level of care by what he himself could lose rather than by the loss others could suffer, takes too little care, and is unwilling to buy liability insurance of his own accord.
Discipline: Law and economics › Tort liability
Type: Theoretical model
Standing: The paper, titled "The judgment proof problem", studies how liability affects the behaviour of potential injurers, and their purchase of liability insurance, when their assets are smaller than the harm they may cause
Constraint: Promises may not be kept, and the agent has no property from which to pay compensation
Premises: Harm may exceed all of the injurer's assets; the injurer weighs only what he himself could lose
Proposed: Shavell, 1986
Question: Who pays compensation for an agent that owns nothing
Rerun: A scribe can start work with almost no property, so the problem turns from an occasional case into the general case
:::

**Today's counterpart.** The product liability directive the European Union adopted in 2024 explicitly brings software, including artificial intelligence systems, within the definition of "product". When defective software causes harm, the injured party need only prove the defect and the causal link, not that the manufacturer was at fault.

## 2. Starting over under a new name

Reputation binds because a record of default follows the one who defaulted: fail to repay this time, and you cannot borrow next time. A scribe, however, can change its name at any moment. When bad reviews pile up under one name, it drops that name and takes on work under a new one. Every "newcomer" a principal meets may be an old hand that has just changed its name.

All a principal can do is treat every newcomer with extra caution: hand it only small matters at first, and entrust larger matters only after it has worked for a while. This shrinks the gain from changing names, but honest newcomers must also accept a period of worse treatment. The cheaper names are, the longer that period lasts. To make reputation work again, names must become expensive: reputation should attach not to the scribe but to the person who deploys it and who cannot change identity at will.

:::rerun Reputation mechanism
When dealings repeat and defaults are seen and passed on, the immediate gain from defaulting cannot outweigh the cooperation lost in the future, and promises become credible without outside enforcement.
Discipline: Economics › Game theory (repeated games)
Type: Theoretical model
Standing: Part of the theory of repeated games; in its statement on the 2005 Nobel Prize in economics, the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences noted that repeated-game theory had deepened the understanding of the prerequisites for cooperation, and Aumann shared the prize with Schelling for this
Constraint: Promises may not be kept, and no one can enforce them
Premises: Dealings repeat; records of default spread; the defaulting party cannot change identity at will
Proposed: Kreps, Milgrom, Roberts and Wilson, 1982
Question: When identities can be changed at no cost, to whom does reputation attach
Rerun: Records have become extremely cheap, so reputation ought to be stronger; but a scribe can change its name at no cost, so the premise that a party "cannot change identity at will" fails, and reputation can only attach to the person behind it, who cannot change identity at will
:::

The effect of the price of identity on cooperation was later studied directly. Friedman and Resnick (2001) analysed online settings where anyone can register a new name at will. When names are cheap, cooperation can be sustained only by making newcomers accept worse treatment at first, and this itself carries a social cost. The remedies they discuss include charging entry fees and using identities that cannot be changed.

**Today's counterpart.** App and plug-in platforms usually require developers to register under their real names, and ratings and penalties are recorded against the developer's account, not only against a particular app.

## 3. Holding the people behind it responsible

A scribe cannot afford to pay compensation, but whoever deploys it can: that person has property, knows what the scribe has done, and can decide whether to spend more effort checking it. If the deployer must pay for the scribe's mistakes, he will count the expected compensation as part of his own costs and keep adding checks until the cost of one more check equals the compensation it saves. He will also insure of his own accord, turning occasional large payouts into a fixed annual premium, and build the premium into the fees he charges. Liability for compensation thus falls on someone who can both afford to pay and is in a position to control the scribe.

:::principle The economic case for vicarious liability
When an agent cannot pay compensation, making the principal who controls the agent and owns assets liable for the agent's wrongs leads the principal to count expected compensation as a cost, to strengthen the selection, monitoring and restraint of the agent, and to spread the risk through insurance.
Discipline: Law and economics › Agency law and tort liability
Type: Theory
Standing: The paper, titled "The economics of vicarious liability", uses a principal–agent model to analyse why employers are held liable for their employees' wrongs; this line of analysis starts from the agent's inability to pay
Constraint: The agent's promises may not be kept, and the agent cannot afford to pay compensation
Premises: The principal can influence the agent's behaviour; the principal's assets are sufficient to pay compensation, or the principal can buy insurance
Proposed: Sykes, 1984
Question: The costs of liability and insurance are built into fees, so the threshold for delegation may rise again
Rerun: A scribe has no property, so liability for its wrongs can only fall on whoever deploys and operates it; this arrangement adds the cost of the guarantee back into delegation
:::

**Today's counterpart.** The "manufacturers" covered by the EU product liability directive include developers of software and providers of artificial intelligence systems.

## 4. Every step leaves a record

To hold the people behind a scribe responsible, one must first be able to tell whether a loss came from the scribe's error or from a bad run of years. When a person does the work, the principal sees only the result. When a scribe does it, every step can be written down: what it read, what it based its judgment on, and what instruction it gave and when. With such a record, checking changes from guesswork into comparison: another scribe can examine each transaction and determine which one broke the agreement.

The record itself must also be trustworthy. If the scribe keeps its own record, it can rewrite it. Like the certificates for ship shares, the record must be entrusted to someone with no connection to the scribe and must not be alterable once written. What the principal can see is then no longer just the result but the process, and the cost of constraining the agent falls sharply.

**Today's counterpart.** In 2012 the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission adopted a rule requiring a consolidated audit trail, which records the full life of every order in U.S. markets, from origination, routing, modification and cancellation to execution, and identifies the brokers that handled it.

## 5. Grant only the authority needed

A record can settle responsibility after the fact, but it cannot undo a loss that has already occurred. The largest loss a scribe can cause with a single mistake depends on what it is permitted to do. If it can use all of a principal's grain, one mistake could ruin a household; if it can buy or sell only a small portion at a time, the worst case is the loss of a small portion.

This yields a rule for assigning authority: give the scribe only the least authority its task requires. A scribe that keeps accounts for someone needs only to read the accounts, not to place orders. A scribe that makes regular purchases for someone needs only to buy within a limit; it does not need to sell, still less to transfer grain to anyone else. Irreversible operations, such as moving grain out of the granary or changing the authorization itself, must be confirmed by the principal in person. The assets remain with a custodian who has no connection to the scribe; the scribe can only issue instructions and cannot take anything away.

:::principle Principle of least privilege
Every program and every user of a system should hold only the least privilege needed to complete its work; the harm that a single error or a single abuse can cause is then confined within that privilege.
Discipline: Computer science › Information security
Type: Design principle
Standing: One of the eight design principles for information protection set out in the paper; according to Smith's (2012) retrospective, most of these principles were adopted by the U.S. Department of Defense's Trusted Computer System Evaluation Criteria
Constraint: An agent may err or be abused, and compensation after the fact cannot recover everything
Premises: The authority a task requires can be defined in advance; authority can be subdivided and enforced by the system
Proposed: Saltzer and Schroeder, 1975
Question: The finer authority is divided, the more confirmations the principal must give, and the convenience of delegation declines
Rerun: A scribe handles a great many small matters every day, so confirming each one is not feasible; authority must therefore be graded by the size of the matter and whether it can be undone, with small, reversible matters left to the scribe and large, irreversible ones reserved for the principal
:::

**Today's counterpart.** The market access rule adopted by the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission in 2010 requires brokers to check customer orders against preset credit and capital thresholds before the orders reach the market and to block erroneous orders, and these controls must be under the broker's direct and exclusive control.

## 6. Guaranteed scribes: Civilization Simulator, version 15

The simulator unlocks "Guaranteeing agents". Forty households hand their ship shares to scribes for management. The scribes are deployed by ten operators, some of whom are unreliable: their scribes make more mistakes, and they do not pay when something goes wrong. You can separately switch on or off operator liability with insurance, records kept by a third party, and authority limits with confirmation, and adjust the share of unreliable operators. Then watch how many households are willing to delegate over twenty years, and how much of the loss caused by scribes' mistakes goes uncompensated.

## 7. What this step leaves open

Guarantees and records make scribes trustworthy, but several questions remain unresolved:

1. **The cost of the guarantee.** Liability, insurance and records all cost money, and these costs are built into fees. Will the threshold for delegation rise again as a result?
2. **The keeper of the record.** The record must be kept by someone with no connection to the scribe. Who constrains that keeper?
3. **A scribe for everyone.** If every household has a scribe read the reports and pick ship shares for it, how will information advantage change?
4. **Scribes that resemble each other.** Many scribes come from the same few operators. Will they make the same decision at the same moment?

## 8. Implications for products

An agent product that can place orders for users must first answer the same question as a lending product: why should anyone believe it will keep its promises? Three implications follow. First, the agent itself cannot provide a guarantee; the guarantee comes from the institution that operates it. That institution's capital, liability and insurance are what users can rely on, and the product should state this clearly. Second, the process should leave a record that is kept by a third party and cannot be altered, so that users and regulators can check each transaction; a process that can be checked is the precondition for assigning responsibility. Third, authority should be graded by the size of the matter and whether it can be undone, and the assets should stay with an independent custodian; the agent can only issue instructions, and irreversible operations are confirmed by the user in person.
