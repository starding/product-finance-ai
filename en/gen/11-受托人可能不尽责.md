> After delegation, the fleet manager holds the whole village's ship shares and knows the details of every ship better than any household. He can quietly sell some of them and keep the grain for himself, or sell the riskiest ship shares to the elderly people least able to bear a loss. When principals cannot tell who is reliable, they will pay only for the average, and reliable managers, finding it not worth their while, leave the trade. Four arrangements grow up in response: assets are kept by someone unconnected with the manager, who can only give instructions; the manager must regularly disclose where the ship shares have gone and what risks they carry; whoever sells ship shares must first find out what the buyer can bear; and someone accepted by all parties checks and punishes. All these arrangements have costs, and the people doing the regulating also need to be constrained.

## 1. The manager holds everyone's ship shares

The fleet manager manages ship shares for forty households. All the share certificates are in his hands, and each year he reports to every household how much more the fleet has earned. But the households cannot see the certificates and do not know whether the shares are still there. If he quietly sells some, keeps the grain for himself and pays old principals their returns out of new principals' grain, the books can be kept up for many years, until one day the incoming grain is not enough to pay out.

There is one new constraint: trustees and sellers know more than principals, and their interests do not fully coincide with the principals'.

**Today's counterpart.** Frauds that pay old investors' returns out of new investors' money have occurred in many countries, and are often discovered only when the flow of money breaks down.

## 2. A market where good cannot be told from bad

If principals cannot tell which managers are reliable, they can only pay at the average level. Reliable managers receive the same as unreliable ones, find it not worth it and leave; among those who remain, a larger share is unreliable, principals are willing to pay even less, and another group of reliable managers leaves. This process repeats round after round, and in the worst case all the reliable managers leave and delegation itself cannot be sustained.

:::principle The market for lemons
When sellers know quality and buyers do not, buyers will pay only for average quality, so sellers of good quality leave, average quality falls, and the market may shrink all the way down to only its worst part.
Discipline: Economics › Information economics
Type: Theoretical model
Standing: The 2001 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences went to Akerlof, Spence and Stiglitz "for their analyses of markets with asymmetric information"; the prize announcement used this paper to explain Akerlof's contribution: when sellers know more about quality than buyers, adverse selection may shrink a market until only low-quality products remain
Constraint: Trustees and sellers know more than principals
Premises: Sellers know quality better than buyers; buyers can bid only for average quality; sellers of good quality have somewhere else to go
Proposed: Akerlof, 1970; the paper uses the used-car market as its example, where a bad used car is commonly called a "lemon" in the United States
Question: How can buyers tell good from bad, or sellers of good quality prove themselves?
:::

The fleet is even harder to judge than a used car. Someone who buys a car will eventually find out whether it is good after driving it for a while. A principal, even after a year, who sees that the fleet lost money cannot tell whether it met a storm or the manager failed in his duty; seeing that the fleet made money, he does not know whether another manager would have made more. Moreover, the person who judges what needs to be done is often the person who does it: if the manager says a ship needs a major overhaul, the principals can only pay. With no way for principals to compare, competition among managers can hardly eliminate this kind of fraud.

:::principle Credence goods
The quality of some goods and services cannot be judged by buyers even after use, only taken on trust from the seller; when the seller is also responsible for judging what the buyer needs, fraud can persist even under full competition.
Discipline: Economics › Information economics
Type: Theory
Standing: Beyond the two kinds of goods whose quality can be judged before purchase or only after use, it proposed a third kind, "credence goods"; the paper is titled "Free competition and the optimal amount of fraud"
Constraint: Trustees and sellers know more than principals and are hard to check even afterwards
Premises: Buyers cannot judge quality even afterwards; the seller both diagnoses and provides the service; verification is very costly
Proposed: Darby and Karni, 1973; at its core, the seller holds more information than the buyer, and diagnosis and service are provided by the same party
Question: Most financial services have this character; how can principals check their trustees?
:::

**Today's counterpart.** When investors lose trust in a whole industry, reliable firms suffer along with the rest, and money leaves the market.

## 3. Custody: assets out of the manager's hands

The most direct remedy is to give the share certificates to someone unconnected with the manager for safekeeping. The manager can decide which ship to buy and which to sell, but can only give instructions to the custodian and cannot take the shares away himself; the custodian acts on the instructions and reports directly to each principal how much is held in their name. Then even if the manager wants to misappropriate, there is nothing he can take, and the figures principals see no longer come only from the manager's mouth.

**Today's counterpart.** Fund assets are held by independent custodian banks. The custody rule amended by the US Securities and Exchange Commission in 2009 requires that client assets managed by investment advisers generally be held with a qualified custodian, that the custodian send account statements directly to clients, and that an independent accountant conduct a surprise examination each year.

## 4. Disclosure: making the invisible visible

Custody ensures the assets are still there, but cannot tell principals whether the manager is doing well or how much risk he is taking. Another remedy is to require the manager to disclose regularly which ships he has bought, how risky each one is and how much he has charged in fees. As long as principals can tell good from bad, reliable managers win more mandates, unreliable ones have nowhere to hide, and the collapse of the lemons market can be avoided. For disclosure to be truthful, an independent party is often also needed to check the accounts.

Greenstone, Oyer and Vissing-Jørgensen (2006) studied the 1964 US legislation that extended mandatory disclosure to over-the-counter companies. They found that the most affected companies earned excess returns of 11.5% to 22.1% relative to matched companies between the bill's proposal and its taking effect, showing that investors valued the additional information.

**Today's counterpart.** Funds must regularly publish their holdings, performance and fees; listed companies must publish audited financial reports.

## 5. Sellers know more: suitability

Another conflict arises on the selling side. The manager earns different commissions on different ship shares; the riskiest shares often carry the highest commissions, and the elderly, who most need safety, are least able to understand the risks involved. If sellers look only at commissions, they will sell unsuitable things to the people they suit least.

Mullainathan, Noeth and Schoar (2012) sent trained auditors posing as clients to consult financial advisers. They found that advisers often failed to correct clients' biases and instead reinforced those biases that worked in the advisers' favour, and tended to recommend actively managed funds with higher fees. Hence the requirement of suitability: sellers must first understand what buyers can bear and what they need, and recommend only products that match.

**Today's counterpart.** Risk-tolerance assessments before opening an account or buying a product, and rules that match products to clients by risk level.

## 6. Who watches the watchers

Custody, disclosure and suitability all need someone to check and enforce them: who confirms that the custodian really keeps the assets, who checks the disclosed accounts, who punishes those who break the rules. That role is the regulator. The stricter the regulation, the less fraud, but every household and every manager pays a cost for it: filling in reports, undergoing inspections, waiting for approvals. When the cost is too high, small managers cannot bear it, and the industry is left with only a few large players.

Who makes the rules also shapes what the rules say. Each of the forty principal households is affected only a little by the rules, so few are willing to spend time pressing for better ones; the few large managers, by contrast, have a great deal at stake and find it easy to band together, so they actively try to influence the rule-makers. The rules may thus drift in favour of the existing managers: requiring newcomers to obtain licences first and limiting competition on fees both keep newcomers out and protect the incumbents.

:::principle Regulatory capture
Regulation, like anything else, has supply and demand; a regulated industry, whose stakes are concentrated and which is easy to organize, can often sway the rules, so that regulation is designed and operated mainly for the industry's own benefit.
Discipline: Economics › Regulatory economics
Type: Theory
Standing: Stigler received the 1982 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences "for his seminal studies of industrial structures, functioning of markets and causes and effects of public regulation"; the prize announcement said his studies of the causes of regulatory legislation opened up an entirely new area of economic research
Constraint: Those who enforce the rules also need to be constrained
Premises: The regulated are few, with large stakes, and easy to organize; the public affected by the rules is numerous, with dispersed stakes
Proposed: Stigler, 1971, in a paper titled "The theory of economic regulation"
Question: How can rule-makers be made accountable to principals rather than to the regulated industry?
:::

The strength of the rules therefore has a suitable range: it must keep losses from fraud small enough, without letting compliance costs swallow the benefits that delegation brings.

**Today's counterpart.** Securities regulators set rules for custody, disclosure and sales conduct and carry out inspections; regulators themselves are accountable to the legislature and subject to public scrutiny.

## 8. What this step leaves open

Rules constrain trustees and sellers, but several questions remain unresolved:

1. **People make their own mistakes.** Even when products are reliable and information is public, principals still chase rising prices, sell into falls and redeem too often, earning less than the products themselves.
2. **Rules can govern only what can be seen.** What is disclosed are past figures; the rules cannot fully see the manager's judgement and motives at this moment.
3. **New trustees.** If the one acting on others' behalf is no longer a person but an agent that can place orders directly, who holds custody over its actions, who checks them, and who is responsible when it makes a mistake?
4. **Whether the regulator itself can be trusted.** Enforcing the rules depends on someone all parties trust, and that trust likewise needs constraints and accountability.

## 9. Implications for products

Custody, information disclosure and suitability management are all now written into financial products themselves. Three implications follow. First, trustworthiness comes from institutions, not just from promises: who holds the assets and who can move them should be clear to users at a glance. Second, the value of disclosure lies in letting users tell good from bad; spelling out risks, fees and conflicts of interest is how a reliable product gets recognized in a market for credence goods. Third, whether a product suits the user matters as much as whether the product itself is excellent; suitability should be designed as a product feature, not treated as a formality before the sale.
