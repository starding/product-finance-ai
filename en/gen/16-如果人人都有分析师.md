> In the past, only a few households could afford the time to gather news and analyse fleets, and those who were informed could earn a little at the expense of those who were not. Now every household has a scribe that can read every report in a few minutes. With more people gathering information, prices reflect it faster and more fully, and the benefit of reading reports thins out until it is almost zero. But the race does not end: whoever learns something a moment earlier can still make money from others, so everyone competes to be first, spending resources that create no value at all. With more information than anyone can read, what becomes scarce is attention, and those who write reports learn to write for scribes. The new asymmetry moves to data that is not public.

## 1. Everyone can read every report

At the step where prices gather information, the share of informed people settled at a point where one more person gathering information would not gain enough to cover the cost. Now only one thing changes: each household's scribe reads the reports and compares the fleets for it, at almost no cost.

Work through how that point moves. As the cost falls, information gathering that used not to pay now pays, and more people gather information. The more people gather it, the more prices reflect it before the news becomes public, and the less is left for each informed person. The new point lies where the benefit equals the very low cost: most households are informed, prices reflect the great majority of what can be read from public reports, and yet each informed person gains almost nothing from it.

:::rerun Grossman–Stiglitz paradox
When acquiring information is costly, prices cannot fully reflect all information; in equilibrium the share of informed traders makes the return from acquiring information just cover its cost, and prices convey only part of what the informed know.
Discipline: Economics › Information economics (financial markets)
Type: Theoretical model
Standing: The paper is titled "On the impossibility of informationally efficient markets", and the contradiction it reveals is commonly called the "Grossman–Stiglitz paradox"; Stiglitz shared the 2001 Nobel Prize in economics with Akerlof and Spence for their analyses of markets with asymmetric information
Constraint: Acquiring information is costly
Premises: Information can be obtained only at a cost; prices reveal part of the informed traders' information to the uninformed; prices also contain noise unrelated to information
Proposed: Grossman and Stiglitz, 1980
Question: Once the cost of reading public reports approaches zero, what information is still worth paying to acquire
Rerun: For information that can be read from public reports, the cost of acquisition approaches zero, the share of informed traders rises sharply, prices reflect most of this information, and reading reports no longer gives an advantage; the paradox's premises still hold only for information that remains costly to acquire
:::

**Today's counterpart.** Research by Lopez-Lira and Tang found that scoring news headlines with a large language model can predict subsequent stock price reactions. Their working paper, revised in 2025, reports that the returns to this strategy declined as large language models spread, which the authors consider consistent with prices becoming more efficient.

## 2. The value of being first

Reading reports no longer gives an advantage, but being first still does. The news that a ship has come into port will always become public, yet whoever knows a moment earlier can buy ship shares before others know and sell them after the news is out. That moment's lead does not make the ship carry one more portion of cargo, nor does it improve anyone's plans. It only moves a sum of money from those who learned later to those who learned earlier.

For each household, being first is worth it; for the village as a whole, the race to be first only consumes resources. Every household hires a faster scribe and stations itself closer to the dock, spending more and more labour, while the sum that changes hands does not grow.

:::principle Private and social value of information
Information that only changes who owns wealth, without changing production or decisions, is valuable to whoever obtains it but may be worth nothing to society as a whole; people therefore invest too many resources in obtaining such information first.
Discipline: Economics › Information economics
Type: Theoretical model
Standing: The paper, titled "The private and social value of information and the reward to inventive activity", shows that the private value of information can exceed its social value, which can even be zero, and uses invention to show that inventive activity may be excessive
Constraint: Everyone wants to make use of information
Premises: The information will become public sooner or later; those who know early can trade before it becomes public; the information itself does not change production
Proposed: Hirshleifer, 1971
Question: How can a market stop rewarding knowledge that is merely a moment earlier
Rerun: Scribes make the speed of understanding news converge, and the race shifts to whose scribe is faster and who is closer to the source of the news; the resources spent on being first may increase as a result
:::

**Today's counterpart.** Budish, Cramton and Shim (2015) studied the speed race in high-frequency trading. Under continuous trading, a tiny speed advantage yields arbitrage profits, so trading firms compete to invest in faster connections. They propose instead to match orders in a batch at very short intervals, to end this race.

## 3. The new scarcity: attention

Scribes can read every report, and they can also write more reports for each household: an analysis of every ship, a reminder every day, every possible opportunity. But a principal has only a little time each day to look at these things. The more information there is, the smaller the chance that any one item is seen. What is truly scarce shifts from information itself to the attention needed to look at it.

Once attention is scarce, whoever decides what the principal sees holds a great deal of power. A scribe that selects and ranks items for the principal can put the most important matter first, or it can put first the matter most favourable to the seller.

:::principle A wealth of information creates a poverty of attention
Information consumes the attention of its recipients; the richer the information, the scarcer attention becomes, and organizations and systems should be designed to economize on recipients' attention rather than to supply more information.
Discipline: Management and economics › Organization and decision theory
Type: Theory
Standing: Simon made this judgment in a paper on designing organizations for an information-rich world; its phrase that "a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention" is often regarded as the origin of the term "attention economy"; Simon received the 1978 Nobel Prize in economics "for his pioneering research into the decision-making process within economic organizations"
Constraint: There is more information than anyone can read, and time to look at it is limited
Premises: Recipients' attention is limited; processing information takes the recipient's time
Proposed: Simon, 1971
Question: In whose interest does a scribe that selects and ranks items for the principal do its ranking
Rerun: Scribes make the production of information almost free, so the scarcity of attention becomes more acute; the power to rank falls into the hands of scribes and those who deploy them
:::

**Today's counterpart.** The home screens of news and investing apps are ranked by algorithms, and whether a given item appears first or twentieth makes a large difference to the chance that it is seen.

## 4. Those being read change too

Shipowners discover that their reports are read first by scribes and only then by principals. Scribes judge prospects by counting the negative words in a report, so shipowners begin to weigh their wording: the same loss, described differently, earns a higher score from the scribe. Reports become easier for scribes to read and better at pleasing them. When the reader changes, the writer changes along with it.

Cao, Jiang, Yang and Zhang (2023) studied the annual and quarterly reports of U.S. listed companies. The more these filings are downloaded and read by machines, the more companies write them to be easy for machines to process and avoid words that count as negative in sentiment dictionaries. The change is especially marked after a dictionary is published and after new language models are released.

**Today's counterpart.** Corporate disclosure now faces two kinds of readers, people and machines. Those being read study those who read them, and the readers in turn must see through the new ways of writing.

## 5. A new asymmetry: data that is not public

Once the advantage of public reports is used up, what advantage remains comes from things the reports do not contain: the voyage records of one's own fleet, informants at the dock, data sold only to a few. Other people's scribes cannot read this information, so prices do not reflect it in advance, and those who hold it can still gain at others' expense. The more widespread scribes become, the less public information is worth and the more exclusive data is worth.

Katona, Painter, Patatoukas and Zeng (2025) studied satellite images of U.S. retailers' car parks. Investors who could buy this data profited by short selling companies with poor results before those companies released their earnings, while individual investors were buying during the same period. The authors conclude that unequal access to data can widen information asymmetry without immediately improving price discovery.

**Today's counterpart.** Alternative data such as satellite images, card transaction records and web traffic is bought mainly by a small number of large institutions.

## 6. The arithmetic does not change

Scribes make active selection much cheaper, but they do not change one thing: all the ship shares held by everyone, taken together, are all the ship shares there are. Before costs, active selectors as a group can only get the average. The extra cost they pay to select becomes smaller, so the margin by which they fall behind narrows, but it does not turn into a lead.

:::rerun The arithmetic of active management
Before costs, the average return of active managers as a group equals that of investors who passively hold everything; after costs, active managers as a group must fall behind.
Discipline: Finance › Investment management
Type: Theorem
Standing: The paper is titled "The arithmetic of active management"; the author states that both assertions hold for any period and depend only on the laws of addition, subtraction, multiplication and division
Constraint: Information advantage is zero-sum among traders
Premises: Passive investors hold all securities in proportion to their market shares; returns are averaged with weights equal to the money invested; active management costs more than passive holding
Proposed: Sharpe, 1991
Question: Among active selectors, who wins and who loses
Rerun: Scribes lower the cost of active management, so the margin by which active investors as a whole fall behind narrows, but as long as their costs remain higher than those of passive holding, the conclusion stands; who wins and who loses within the group now depends on who holds non-public data and who is faster
:::

**Today's counterpart.** Stock selection tools built on large language models have greatly reduced the cost of active selection, but index products that hold everything in proportion to market share remain the cheaper choice.

## 8. What this step leaves open

Once everyone has an analyst, several questions remain unresolved:

1. **Analysts that resemble each other.** Many scribes come from the same few operators, read the same reports and use the same methods. Will they reach the same judgment at the same moment?
2. **The race to be first.** Once the speed of understanding news converges, the race shifts to who is faster and who is closer to the source of the news. How can a market stop rewarding knowledge that is merely a moment earlier?
3. **The power to rank.** In whose interest does a scribe that selects and ranks items for the principal do its ranking?
4. **Exclusive data.** Non-public data is becoming ever more valuable. Who holds it, and to whom should it be disclosed?

## 9. Implications for products

Analysis tools that everyone can use are turning the reading of reports into a basic capability. Three implications follow. First, drawing conclusions from public information is no longer a product's advantage; a product's value must shift elsewhere: helping users stick to their plans, checking agents, and controlling risk and cost. Second, when a product selects and ranks information for users, what it holds is the users' attention; ranking by the user's goals rather than by who pays is the precondition for such a product to be trustworthy. Third, do not promise users that they can beat the market with public information. Real information advantage comes from non-public data and greater speed, and ordinary users usually have neither.
