> Once the agreement was settled, the villager went back to his fields. He has only a few evenings a year to look at the accounts, yet the scribe has work to do every day: making deposits, adjusting holdings, deciding after a storm whether to sell, deciding when he falls ill whether to touch the house money. If it asks him about everything, he is worn down and the scribe is of no use; if it never asks, he loses control of his own property. The agreement cannot cover every case, and who decides the cases it leaves out must be made clear in advance. The agreement must also state goals, boundaries, rules, exceptions and term; authority must be graded by how large a matter is and whether it can be undone; and when the scribe must come back and ask him must be written in as well.

## 1. He cannot be present all the time

Every day the scribe may meet something that calls for a decision. If it asks him about each one, he will be interrupted more than a hundred times a year and will soon stop reading, stop answering, or nod without thinking. If it asks about none, every decision it makes for him rests only on its own judgment. Between the two lies a sensible position: the scribe handles small, routine matters itself under the agreement, and brings the few important ones back to him.

There is one new constraint: the user cannot be present all the time, so continuing delegation depends on an agreement made in advance.

**Today's counterpart.** In a discretionary account, the client gives written authorization in advance, and the fiduciary decides purchases and sales on its own within the agreed scope. In a non-discretionary account, every trade requires the client's confirmation.

## 2. The agreement cannot be complete

However detailed the agreement, it cannot foresee everything: the fleet changes its route, the village adopts a new rule, a child is born into his family, or something happens that nobody expected. Someone has to decide the cases the agreement does not cover. Work through who should hold that decision. Give it to the scribe, and it can adapt to circumstances, but it may not know what he cares about. Keep it with him, and he knows what he cares about, but he may not be there in time. The decision can go to only one side first, so it must be settled in advance which uncovered cases the scribe handles in the spirit of the agreement and which must wait for his return.

:::principle Incomplete contracts and residual control rights
A contract cannot specify every contingency in advance; who holds the right to decide in the cases the contract does not cover, that is, the residual control rights, affects both parties' investment beforehand and their behaviour afterwards, and is therefore an important arrangement in its own right.
Discipline: Economics › Contract theory
Type: Theoretical model
Standing: The paper defines ownership as residual control rights and uses this to analyse the boundaries of the firm; later literature names Grossman, Hart and Moore together as the founders of research on incomplete contracts; Hart and Holmström received the 2016 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences "for their contributions to contract theory"
Constraint: Continuing delegation requires an agreement in advance, and the agreement cannot be complete
Premises: Writing down every contingency is too costly; the residual right to decide can belong to only one party at a time
Proposed: Grossman and Hart, 1986; Hart and Moore, 1990
Question: On what basis should the residual right to decide go to the scribe or stay with the principal
:::

**Today's counterpart.** FINRA's rule on discretionary accounts requires that, before a fiduciary exercises discretion, the client has given prior written authorization and the firm has accepted the account in writing. Where the authority covers only the time and price of execution, it is generally valid only for that day.

## 3. What the agreement should contain

That the agreement cannot be complete is no reason to leave it unwritten. Work out what the villager must write down at the least: what he wants to achieve (goals, namely the three accounts); what must not be crossed (boundaries: the share of ship shares in each account, the maximum loss, the fee ceiling, what may not be bought); how things are done in the ordinary course (rules: when to deposit, when to adjust, how far a deviation may go before holdings are brought back); when exceptions apply (which situations require stopping to ask him); and how long the agreement lasts and how it can be revoked. Goals and boundaries tell the scribe where to go and where it must not go; rules mean most everyday matters need no request; exceptions and term let him keep final control.

**Today's counterpart.** The elements of an investment policy statement that the CFA Institute sets out for individual investors include scope and purpose; governance (who drafts, implements and monitors the investment policy); return and risk objectives and constraints; and risk management and rebalancing.

## 4. Grading authority

Not everything is worth asking him about, and not everything can go unasked. Sort matters by two questions: how large is this, and can a mistake be undone? Size can be measured by the amount involved as a share of the household's property; for example, no more than 2% counts as small, and once trust is established the threshold can be raised. Small and reversible matters, such as scheduled regular deposits or bringing holdings back to the agreed proportions, the scribe handles on its own and simply records afterwards. Large and reversible matters, such as a sizeable adjustment, it carries out and then promptly tells him about, and he can reverse them. Small and irreversible matters, such as selling a few ship shares to pay a small fee, it also carries out first within the scope of the agreement and promptly tells him about. Large and irreversible matters, such as moving grain out of the granary, drawing early on the house or retirement money, or changing the agreement itself, require his own confirmation in advance. Anything outside the agreement's boundaries, such as buying a product leveraged with borrowed money, is forbidden whatever its size. This gives four levels: act on its own; act, then tell; confirm first; and forbidden.

:::rerun Principle of least privilege
Every program and every user of a system should hold only the least privilege needed to do its work; the damage that one error or one abuse can cause is then confined within that privilege.
Discipline: Computer science › Information security
Type: Design principle
Standing: One of eight design principles for information protection set out in the paper; according to Smith's (2012) review, most of these principles were adopted by the US Department of Defense's Trusted Computer System Evaluation Criteria
Constraint: An agent may err or be misused, and compensation after the fact cannot undo everything
Premises: The privileges a task requires can be defined in advance; privileges can be subdivided and enforced by the system
Proposed: Saltzer and Schroeder, 1975
Question: Who sets the boundaries between levels, and how should they change as trust grows
Rerun: In a product, privilege is not simply "allowed or not allowed". It is graded by the size of the matter and whether it can be undone into four levels, act on its own; act, then tell; confirm first; and forbidden, written into the agreement and enforced by the system
:::

**Today's counterpart.** SAE International's standard for levels of driving automation defines six levels, from 0 to 5, and specifies that the level depends on the features engaged at the time; the same vehicle can operate at different levels in different situations.

## 5. Letting go and keeping control

The agreement says "ask him about the big things", but he knows far less than the scribe: the scribe watches the fleet and the accounts every day, while he looks only a few times a year. When the scribe proposes a plan, he can usually only nod. The formal right to decide stays with him, but more and more of the real decision passes to the scribe. Letting go has benefits: the scribe will look for opportunities and deal with problems more actively. The cost is that he loses control. The less time he spends understanding the situation, the more pronounced the shift becomes.

:::principle Formal and real authority
The party holding the right to decide (formal authority) does not necessarily control the decision in practice (real authority); real authority depends on who holds the information. The more real authority the agent has, the stronger its incentive to take initiative, and the less control the principal keeps over decisions.
Discipline: Economics › Organizational economics (contract theory)
Type: Theoretical model
Standing: The paper distinguishes formal from real authority, shows that real authority is determined by the information structure, which in turn depends on how formal authority is allocated, and analyses factors that raise a subordinate's real authority, such as overload, lenient rules, urgency of decisions, reputation and performance measurement
Constraint: The principal cannot be present all the time and has less information than the agent
Premises: Acquiring information takes time and effort; the preferences of principal and agent are not fully aligned
Proposed: Aghion and Tirole, 1997
Question: How can one let go and still keep real control
:::

**Today's counterpart.** The choice between a discretionary and a non-discretionary account is, at bottom, a trade-off between initiative and control.

## 6. When to come back and ask him

The key to letting go while keeping control is to write into the agreement when the scribe must come back and ask him, rather than leaving it wholly to the scribe's judgment. There can be three kinds of trigger. The first is quantitative: an account has fallen by more than the agreed amount, or has drifted too far from its target. The second is an event: he changes jobs, a child is born, someone falls ill. The third is time: a scheduled review once a year, even if nothing has happened. Once a trigger fires, the scribe must set out the situation, the options and its recommendation, and he decides.

**Today's counterpart.** Among the elements of an investment policy statement listed by the CFA Institute, the governance section requires stating how the investment policy is reviewed and updated.

## 7. Ten years in his absence: Civilization Simulator, version 21

The simulator unlocks "mandate parameters and authority". Over ten years the villager meets all kinds of events: storms, a change of job, a new child, illness, a new village rule. You can switch on or off the agreement's exception clauses, graded authority and scheduled reviews, and set how often he can respond to requests. Then see, over the ten years, which matters the scribe handled well on its own, which it should have asked about and did not, which it asked about to no effect, and how many times he was interrupted along the way.

## 8. What this step leaves open

The agreement and the grading of authority let the scribe act while he is away, but he still cannot see what it has done:

1. **He cannot read the accounts.** Every action of the scribe is recorded, but he looks only a few times a year. Can he understand what he sees?
2. **He has no way to check.** The scribe says it kept to the agreement. Why should he believe it?
3. **A nod is not consent.** Does a nod given without understanding the situation count as genuine confirmation?
4. **Agents make mistakes.** Who bears the consequences of an error made within the scope of authority?

## 9. Implications for products

For a wealth-management product built around an agent, the second part that has to grow is "mandate parameters and authority". Three implications follow. First, make the agreement part of the product: goals, boundaries, rules, exceptions and term, written in words the user understands and enforced by the system, rather than a mere disclaimer. Second, grade authority by the size of a matter and whether it can be undone: the agent handles small, reversible matters on its own and records them afterwards; large, irreversible ones require confirmation in advance; and the boundaries between levels can be adjusted as trust grows. Third, write into the agreement when to come back and ask the user: quantitative, event and time triggers; once a trigger fires, set out the situation, options and recommendation and let the user decide, so that the real decision does not pass entirely to the agent without anyone noticing.
