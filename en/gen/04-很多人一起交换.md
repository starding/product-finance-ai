> Once there are many people, exchange runs into a difficulty: if I have grain and want salt, I must find someone who happens to have salt and happens to want grain. This double coincidence of wants arises not only with goods but also with time: a young person wants to keep today's harvest for old age, but cannot find anyone able to repay him decades later. One thing that everyone accepts solves both difficulties: an exchange only has to match on one side, and saving no longer has to wait for a particular borrower. Money is at heart a record of who has given to others; its value rests on people's belief that the next person will accept it, and on discipline in issuing it.

## 1. With many people, wants rarely match

There are more households on the island now, and more kinds of produce: some grow grain, some dry salt, some weave cloth, some fish. I have grain and want salt; the salt-maker I meet wants cloth. An exchange can be completed directly only when the other person happens to have what I want and happens to want what I have. The more kinds of goods there are, the smaller the chance that any person one meets is a match. Jevons (1875) called this situation the double coincidence of wants and noted that it seldom occurs.

It should be said that this is a logical derivation, not history. Anthropologists point out that no society has been found that first relied on pure barter and then developed money out of it (Humphrey, 1985; Graeber, 2011); among acquaintances, keeping accounts and extending credit was more common. This is consistent with seeing money as a kind of record: money and bookkeeping were from the start two forms of the same thing.

**Today's counterpart.** Second-hand goods are rarely swapped directly for other goods, because it is too hard to find someone who needs exactly what the other has. Selling first and then buying uses money to split one exchange into two.

## 2. One thing that everyone accepts

Suppose there is something that everyone is willing to accept first and then trade for what they really want. An exchange then only has to match on one side: it is enough that the other person has what I want, without also wanting what I have. No one has to designate this thing. The islanders will find that some goods change hands more easily than others: salt keeps well and divides easily, while fish rots within days. Someone who trades grain for salt knows that, even if he cannot use that much salt himself, he can later trade the salt for something else. So more and more people accept salt, and salt changes hands ever more easily. Which good wins out depends both on whether it keeps well, divides easily and is hard to counterfeit, and on whether people believe others will accept it. As long as everyone believes others will take shells, shells can circulate, even though they are of no use in themselves; once people stop believing it, shells are just shells.

:::principle Spontaneous emergence of a medium of exchange
The good that changes hands most easily is accepted by more and more people, and the more people accept it the more easily it changes hands, until it becomes the general medium of exchange; which good wins depends both on its own properties and on people's beliefs.
Discipline: Economics › Monetary economics (search models)
Type: Theoretical model
Standing: Kiyotaki and Wright's model, generally known as the Kiyotaki–Wright model, has the medium of exchange arise within the model in an economy where people meet at random and can only trade one-to-one; one of the paper's conclusions is that fiat money, of no use in itself, can also circulate in equilibrium
Constraint: Once there are many people, what each needs rarely lines up exactly
Premises: People each specialise in production and meet at random; goods differ in how well they keep; people form shared beliefs about whether others will accept a given good
Proposed: Menger, 1892, proposed that money emerges spontaneously from the goods that change hands most easily; Kiyotaki and Wright, 1989, gave a formal model
Question: The same good is money under one belief and not under another; what sustains the belief
:::

**Today's counterpart.** The more merchants accept a payment method, the more people are willing to use it; the more people use it, the more merchants accept it.

## 3. Unit of account: a common yardstick

Money has another use: expressing the value of every good in terms of one thing. Without money, every pair of goods needs its own rate of exchange, and ten goods mean forty-five rates to remember; with money, ten prices are enough. Jevons (1875) summed up the uses of money as four: a medium of exchange, a common measure of value, a standard of deferred payment and a store of value.

**Today's counterpart.** Price tags, account balances and financial statements can be compared and added up only because they are all denominated in the same currency.

## 4. Money is a record

Look at one exchange again: I give grain to the salt-maker, and he gives me a few shells. The shells cannot be eaten; what they record is a fact: I once gave grain to someone, and so I am entitled to take back something of equal value from someone else. Imagine the village keeps a complete ledger recording how much each person has given to others and how much each has taken from others. The salt-maker need only open the ledger and see that I have given more than I have taken, and he will be willing to give me salt. Whatever the ledger can do, shells can do; whatever shells can do, the ledger can do too. The advantage of shells is that they are cheap: no one needs to know every dealing in the village, only how many shells the other person holds.

:::principle Money is memory
Where people cannot make credible commitments, any exchange arrangement that can be achieved with money can also be achieved with a complete record of everyone's history of dealings; money is a convenient form of such a record.
Discipline: Economics › Monetary economics
Type: Theoretical model
Standing: The paper is titled "Money is memory"; its main proposition is that whatever arrangement money can achieve, memory can also achieve. The reverse does not necessarily hold: in some environments memory can achieve arrangements that money cannot
Constraint: Once there are many people, it is hard to remember who has given to whom
Premises: People cannot make credible commitments; the record is complete, and everyone can see all past dealings of the person they deal with
Proposed: Kocherlakota, 1998
Question: Who keeps the complete record, and how is it protected from tampering; when the record is incomplete, which is better, money or the record
:::

This is the other side of the reputation for keeping one's word. Reputation is a record of whether a person keeps agreements; money is a record of how much a person has given to others. The cheaper and more reliable the record, the more exchanges can be carried out on the strength of it.

**Today's counterpart.** Most money today is no longer physical; it is numbers in bank accounts, that is, entries in records.

## 5. Across a lifetime: old age for the last generation

Lending requires that the borrower still have income in future, but the youngest generation cannot find anyone younger to lend to. Money can solve this problem. The young sell their harvest to the old for money; when they themselves are old, they use the money to buy grain from the next young generation. No generation has to find a particular borrower; it only has to believe that the next generation will accept money. The same result can also be achieved by an agreement, without money at all: each young generation supports the old of its day, and is in turn supported by the next generation when it grows old.

:::principle Money in the overlapping-generations model
When generations overlap, people earn while young but not when old, and no borrower can be found who will repay in future, a money accepted by one generation after another, or a social agreement under which each young generation supports the old of its day, can ensure that every generation has food in old age, leaving every generation better off than if each stored for itself.
Discipline: Economics › Macroeconomics (overlapping-generations models)
Type: Theoretical model
Standing: Generally known as the overlapping-generations model. In the paper, titled "An exact consumption-loan model of interest with or without the social contrivance of money", the competitive equilibrium in which each generation decides for itself need not be optimal, and introducing money or a social agreement can make every generation better off
Constraint: There is no harvest in old age, and the youngest generation cannot find anyone younger to lend to
Premises: Generations keep overlapping, with no last generation; each generation believes the next will accept money or honour the agreement
Proposed: Samuelson, 1958
Question: Once belief wavers, the chain unravels from its last link; when population and income growth slow, how much can this agreement give each generation
:::

This is also where the arrangement is weak: it depends entirely on belief. If people think the next generation will not accept money, money is worthless today, and the chain unravels from its last link.

Civilization Simulator, version 4, works this chain out. I begin farming at 20, and the second person is twenty years younger than I am. With lending, the grain I have stored in old age can be lent to the second person while he is farming, and no longer spoils. But when he in turn grows old, there is no one younger on the island, and he must rely on storage that spoils. Adding a third person only pushes the problem back one generation: as long as lives are finite, there is always a generation with no one to lend to. Turn on money, with people believing the next generation will accept it, and even the youngest generation can provide for old age; turn off that belief, and everything falls back to relying on lending and storage alone.

**Today's counterpart.** A pay-as-you-go pension is the social agreement Samuelson described: those working today pay contributions that fund those retired today.

## 6. The cost of money: belief and discipline in issuing it

The value of money comes from people's belief that it can be exchanged for goods, and also from its limited quantity. If whoever issues it can add money at will while the island's produce does not grow to match, the same amount of money buys less: prices rise. For those holding money, rising prices are like grain rotting in the store: what they have stored is shrinking. Fisher (1911) described the relation between the quantity of money and prices with the equation of exchange: with the velocity of circulation and the volume of trade unchanged, an increase in the quantity of money raises prices in the same proportion.

**Today's counterpart.** Inflation erodes the purchasing power of cash; central bank independence and inflation targets both exist to maintain discipline in issuing money.

## 7. What this step leaves open

With money, exchange and saving have become much easier, but several problems remain unsolved:

1. **Harvests are uncertain.** Money makes saving more convenient but cannot prevent crop failure; one bad year can still ruin a household.
2. **Maturities do not match.** Savers want to be able to withdraw at any time, while those clearing land or planting orchards need to borrow for many years. How are the two to be matched?
3. **The value of money depends on belief and on the issuer.** Who ensures discipline in issuing, and who ensures the ledger is not tampered with?
4. **Who sets prices.** With a common yardstick in place, prices still have to be determined jointly by the buying and selling of many people.

## 8. Implications for products

Account, payment and cash-management products are all built on the mechanisms of this chapter. Three implications follow. First, the value of payments and accounts comes from the network: the more people accept them, the more useful they are, and in turn the harder they are to replace. Second, money and account balances are at heart records, and the reliability of the record determines its value; keeping records and preventing tampering is the core responsibility of such products. Third, holding money is not without risk: when issuance loses its discipline, cash shrinks like rotting grain. In arranging savings for users, purchasing power must be considered, not just the amount.
