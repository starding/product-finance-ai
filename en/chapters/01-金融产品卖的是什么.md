> A financial product delivers not a good but a promise: money today in exchange for money in the future, under certain circumstances. Every promise is made of three elements: time, risk and trust. The return an investor actually receives from that promise can be split into five terms: time compensation, risk compensation, information advantage, trading costs and luck, on the premise that the promise is kept. The core of financial product design is to state the promise clearly, match it to the user's needs, and make it credible.

## 1. Today's money for tomorrow's promise

When you buy a loaf at a bakery, you get the bread as you pay, and you know on the spot whether it tastes good. When you put money into a fixed-term deposit at a bank, what you get after paying is only a promise: in three years the bank will return the principal with interest.

Every financial product has this structure: pay now, receive later under certain circumstances. Understanding a financial product means answering three questions:

1. **When is it paid out?** When does the money paid today come back? This is time.
2. **How much is paid in which circumstances?** The future has many possibilities; how much does the promise pay in each? This is risk.
3. **Who pays, and why believe them?** Who is making the promise, and do they have the ability and the will to keep it? This is trust.

## 2. The shape of the promise

The future holds more than one possibility, so a promise must state how much it pays in each. Arrow proposed in 1953 (the English translation was published in 1964) that a security can be understood as "a right to payment in different states of the world". From this angle, financial products differ mainly in the shape of their promise:

- **Fixed claims.** Deposits and bonds promise a fixed amount; as long as the promisor does not fail, the outcome is certain.
- **Residual claims.** Shares promise no amount at all. Shareholders receive what is left after the company has kept all its promises to employees, suppliers, creditors and others: a great deal when business is good, possibly nothing when it is bad.
- **Contingent claims.** Insurance and options pay only when a specified event occurs, such as an accident, or a price crossing a certain level.

The creditors and shareholders of the same company hold two promises of different shapes.

{{fig:ch01-claims}}

On this basis Merton pointed out in 1974 that equity is economically equivalent to an option on the value of the company: the higher the company's value, the more shareholders receive; when the company's value is not enough to repay its debts, shareholders' losses are limited to what they put in. Creditors, in effect, give up all gains from company value above the debt in exchange for a fixed promise.

Financial products do not make risk disappear; they only redistribute it among participants. Depositors pass risk to the bank, creditors leave the residual fluctuation to shareholders, and policyholders transfer the losses from accidents to the insurer. The party that bears more risk demands a higher expected return as compensation. This is the **risk premium**.

Put the same money into promises of different shapes, and the distributions of outcomes differ greatly.

{{fig:ch01-promise}}

Three points can be read from the figure:

1. **The more fixed the promise, the more concentrated the outcome, and the lower the average return.** The outcome of a deposit or a government bond is a single point; the outcome of shares is a wide range, with the highest average. The higher return is compensation for bearing greater uncertainty.
2. **A fixed promise is not a fixed outcome.** A corporate bond promises more interest than a government bond, at the cost of possible default by the issuer, which gives the distribution of outcomes a long downward tail. Measured in purchasing power, even a deposit can "lose money".
3. **The median outcome of shares is below the mean.** In most cases the outcome falls below average; a few especially good cases pull the mean up. The longer the horizon, the larger the gap. Presenting a product by its average return alone will leave most users' expectations unmet.

## 3. What capital markets do: the functional perspective

Banks, exchanges, funds and insurers take different forms, and those forms keep changing. Merton and Bodie proposed in 1995 that the financial system is better understood through its functions than through its institutions. Institutions change; functions are comparatively stable. The financial system has six core functions.

{{fig:ch01-functions}}

The functional perspective offers a stable standard for judging anything new: which function does a new product form perform better or more cheaply? Once AI enters financial products, it should be measured by the same standard: which function it improves, at what cost, and whether the improvement holds up under measurement.

## 4. Implications for product design

Design the product around the promise: first state who promises, what is promised, and in which circumstances it fails, then match each of the three elements, time, risk and trust, to the user's needs.
