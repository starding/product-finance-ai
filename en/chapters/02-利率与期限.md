> The first term of the return equation is time compensation: holding today's money until the future deserves compensation in itself. The interest rate is the price of this exchange across time, set jointly by people's patience and by investment opportunities. True compensation must be measured in purchasing power; and when the time a promise pays out differs from the time the money is needed, the compensation is discounted.

## 1. Why waiting must be rewarded

Over a lifetime, a person's income and spending do not line up in time. Income is low in youth, exceeds spending in middle age, and nearly disappears after retirement. Firms are the same: building a plant or doing research requires spending now, and the returns arrive years later.

So the market has two kinds of people: those who have spare money now and are willing to defer consumption, and those who need money now and will be able to repay later. Letting them trade makes both better off. Fisher made this clear in *The Theory of Interest* in 1930: the interest rate is the price of this exchange across time, determined on one side by how long people are willing to wait, and on the other by how much the money would earn if put into production. The life-cycle hypothesis proposed by Modigliani and Brumberg in 1954 added that people tend to smooth consumption over their lives, saving when income is high and borrowing or drawing on savings when it is low.

## 2. Nominal and real returns

The rate written on a deposit slip is the nominal interest rate; it promises how many units of currency you will get back. But a unit of currency in the future may buy less than it does today. Fisher also pointed out that the reward for waiting should be measured by the real interest rate after inflation: roughly the nominal rate minus the inflation rate. With a nominal rate of 2% and inflation of 3%, the amount on deposit grows while its purchasing power shrinks.

{{fig:ch02-real}}

This means the very phrase "capital protected" needs a definition: is it the nominal amount that is protected, or the purchasing power?

## 3. Maturity: when the promise pays and when the money is needed

Maturity is the other face of time compensation. If the money is needed before the promise pays out, the only options are early withdrawal or a sale in the market, often at a discount; if the promise pays out too early, the money sits idle somewhere with a lower return. Whether a product's maturity matches when the user needs the money is the first design question for any financial product.
