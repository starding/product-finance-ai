> A financial agent nominally works for the user, yet it is provided by the platform, paid for by the platform, and trained and evaluated against objectives the platform sets. If the platform's revenue comes from trading volume and time spent in the app, that interest will be written into the agent's objective: one more alert, one more trade, one more paid service. Disclosing the conflict does not remove it; research has even found that after disclosure, advice may become more biased, while those receiving it do not discount it accordingly. The more reliable remedy is structural: revenue that does not rise or fall with trading volume, an agent whose objective is measured by the user's net value, and independent audit. When the agent errs, liability should fall on the party that could have avoided the loss at the lowest cost; and to apportion liability, every step must first have a complete record.

## 1. Whom the agent works for

The classic principal–agent relationship has a single principal. A financial agent faces at least several parties at once: the user who uses it, the platform that provides and operates it, the issuers who sell products on the platform, and the company that supplies the underlying model. Bernheim and Whinston (1986) called the case of one agent serving several principals at once common agency: each party influences the agent's behaviour by paying it or setting its objectives, and the agent's eventual choice is a balance struck among these influences. The user is the nominal principal, yet is often not the party that pays most directly or has the most influence on the objective.

## 2. The conflict is written into the objective

An AI agent's behaviour is determined by the objectives against which it is set up and evaluated. Holmström and Milgrom (1991) showed that when an agent's work has several aspects and only some are measured and rewarded, the agent shifts effort toward the part that is measured. If the platform evaluates the agent by trading volume, clicks and time spent, the agent will send alerts more often and recommend trades more actively; gains in these measures do not mean better outcomes for the user. In 2021 the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission sought public comment on the digital engagement practices of brokers and investment advisers, and its concern was precisely the effect of gamified design, push notifications and prompts on investor behaviour.

## 3. Why disclosure is not enough

The most common remedy is to tell users about the conflict of interest. Yet experiments by Cain, Loewenstein and Moore (2005) found that disclosure can backfire: after disclosing, advisers felt they had met their duty to inform and gave even more biased advice; those receiving the advice knew of the conflict but did not discount the advice enough. As a result, after disclosure the advised party ended up worse off. Disclosure is necessary, but it hands the burden of recognizing the conflict to the people least able to recognize it.

## 4. Structural alignment

More reliable than disclosure is to make conflicts structurally hard to arise. First, the fee model: the agent's revenue does not vary with the number of trades or the products recommended, as with a fixed subscription. Second, objective constraints: the agent is evaluated on the user's net value and its adherence to the mandate, not on trading volume and time spent. Third, information separation: when making a recommendation, the agent cannot see how much revenue each product brings the platform. Fourth, independent audit: regular checks on whether the agent's recommendations lean systematically toward options that favour the platform. In 2023 the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission proposed a rule requiring brokers and investment advisers to identify and eliminate conflicts of interest in predictive data analytics; the proposal was withdrawn in 2025, but the question it raised is exactly the one AI agents must answer.

## 5. Who is responsible when it goes wrong

Agents err for different reasons: the agent misread the mandate, a data source supplied a wrong price, the execution layer failed to block an instruction that crossed a limit, or the agent followed the user's explicit instruction and the result was poor. Calabresi (1970) proposed that liability for an accident should fall on the party able to avoid it at the lowest cost. By this principle, losses from execution beyond the limits should be borne by the platform, because it controls the execution layer's checks; data errors are borne by the party that supplied the data; if the agent misreads a clearly written mandate, the platform bears it; and if the agent follows the user's own explicit instruction and the result falls short of expectations, the user bears it. Liability follows control: whoever can avoid the loss is responsible for it.

{{fig:ch27-liability}}

## 6. Liability must be traceable

Apportioning liability requires being able to reconstruct afterwards what happened: what data the agent saw at the time, which clause of the mandate it relied on, whose authorization it had, and what checks the execution layer performed. The securities industry has long had record-keeping requirements; for example, the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission's Rule 17a-4 specifies the records brokers must keep and how they must keep them. For an AI agent, the record must cover every key action: the input data and its point in time, the clause relied on, the level of authority, and the result of execution. Only with a complete record can liability be apportioned, compensation be grounded, and the same error be avoided.

{{fig:ch27-trace}}

## 7. Implications for product design

Put the agent's revenue and objective on the user's side, and handle conflicts through structure rather than disclosure; liability follows control and rests on a complete record.
